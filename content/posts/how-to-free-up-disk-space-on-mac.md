<h2>How to free up disk space on Mac in ten minutes</h2>
<p>Start where Apple puts the answer: the Storage screen. Click the Apple menu in the top-left corner, choose System Settings, click General in the sidebar, then click Storage. On macOS Ventura 13 or later that path works as written. Older Macs use Apple menu &gt; About This Mac &gt; Storage instead. Watch the bar update as you clear things: the free space figure grows with every deletion.</p>
<p>Read the coloured bar before you delete anything. The bar shows which kinds of files take the most room, and that order differs on every Mac. Below the bar, categories name the same groups in words. Beside several of them, a small info button opens a list of the actual files inside. Sort that list by size, and check the date you last opened each item.</p>
<p>Set a target before you start. Most people clear space for one reason: an update that will not download, a video project that needs room, or a disk-almost-full warning. Knowing the reason tells you how much to free. Anything you delete stays recoverable until you empty the Trash, so a mistake is fixable for a while.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Click the Apple menu, then System Settings, then General, then Storage.</li><li>Note the free space figure at the top of the bar.</li><li>Work through the recommendations, then the categories below.</li><li>Empty the Trash last, and check the free space figure again.</li></ol>

<h2>Turn on the three automatic settings first</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse1.mm.bing.net/th?q=macOS+System+Settings+General+Storage+screen&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="macOS System Settings General Storage screen" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>Three recommendations sit at the top of the Storage screen, and they do the boring work for you. They are Store in iCloud, Optimize Storage, and Empty Trash Automatically. Each has a button beside it. Your Mac shows only the ones that fit your setup, so do not worry if one is missing.</p>
<p>Store in iCloud moves your Desktop and Documents folders into iCloud Drive, and your photos and videos into iCloud Photos. When space runs short, macOS keeps only the files you opened recently on the Mac and downloads the rest when you need them. Anything stored only in iCloud needs an internet connection to open, and it counts against your iCloud storage plan.</p>
<p>Optimize Storage removes Apple TV movies and TV shows you already watched. It also keeps only recent email attachments on the Mac when space is needed. You can download both again later, so nothing is lost for good. Empty Trash Automatically erases anything that has sat in the Trash for more than 30 days. You will find the same setting in the Finder, under Finder &gt; Settings &gt; Advanced.</p>

<div class="note"><b>Tip:</b> Turn on Empty Trash Automatically first. It is the only recommendation with no downside for most people, and it stops the Trash quietly growing into gigabytes.</div>
<h2>Delete the apps and files you no longer use</h2>
<p>Apps are the easiest big win, because one unused app can hold several gigabytes. On the Storage screen, click the info button beside Applications. Every app appears in the list with its size. Select one you no longer use and click Delete. Check the size column first: a small utility is not worth the click, while a game or video editor you stopped using is.</p>
<p>Documents come next, and the same screen makes the hunt short. Click the info button beside Documents, then sort by size and look at the largest files. Old disk images, installers, and exported videos are the usual suspects. Files ending in .dmg are disk images that install software. Once the app is installed, the .dmg is safe to delete.</p>
<p>Your Downloads folder is where installers and attachments pile up out of sight. Reach it from the Dock, from the Finder sidebar, or through Go &gt; Downloads in the Finder menu bar. Sort by size with View &gt; as List, then click the Size column. When you cannot identify a file, open it before deleting, never after.</p>
<ul><li>Installed app: delete the .dmg installer once the app works.</li><li>Exported video: keep the finished file, delete the raw exports.</li><li>Old document: move it to an external drive instead of deleting it.</li></ul>

<h2>Clear the media hogs: photos, videos and messages</h2>
<p>Photos and videos fill a Mac faster than anything else, and the Photos app holds the fix. Open Photos, choose Photos &gt; Settings, click iCloud, and turn on Optimize Mac Storage. Full-resolution originals stay in iCloud Photos, while smaller versions stay on the Mac for browsing. This switch only helps if you use iCloud Photos.</p>
<p>Messages quietly stores every photo and video anyone ever sent you. On the Storage screen, click the info button beside Messages and sort by size. Large attachments sit at the top, and you can delete the ones from conversations that are long over. Keep the ones tied to memories or records you still need.</p>
<p>Mail hides its own weight in attachments and junk. In the Mail app, choose Mailbox &gt; Erase Junk Mail, then Mailbox &gt; Erase Deleted Items. If you back up an iPhone or iPad to this Mac, old backups can also eat large amounts of space. Find them in the Finder with the device connected, and delete backups for devices you no longer own. Your Photos or iMovie library can move to an external drive instead. Copy it first, open it from the new drive to check it works, and only then delete the Mac copy.</p>
<div class="note warn"><b>Warning:</b> Check a moved library opens from the new drive before you delete the original. A copy you never tested is not a backup.</div>
<h2>What System Data is and what to do about it</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse1.mm.bing.net/th?q=Mac+Storage+settings+bar+chart+with+System+Data+segment+highlighted&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="Mac Storage settings bar chart with System Data segment highlighted" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>System Data is the category that confuses everyone, because it sounds like something you can delete. Apple defines it as the storage used by Apple and third-party files that do not belong to a more specific category. That covers temporary files, caches, logs, and data that apps store while they work. That number on its own is not a fault.</p>
<p>Restarting is the safest first move against a swollen System Data figure. Temporary files clear when macOS shuts down cleanly, and the number often drops after a restart. Give the Mac a few minutes after it comes back up, then check the Storage screen again. If the figure fell, the files were temporary and the problem is solved.</p>
<p>Leave the Library folder alone. It holds settings and working files for your apps, and deleting the wrong item can break an app or lose data. The folders inside ~/Library/Caches look like junk, but caches rebuild themselves and deleting them rarely frees space for long. Caches are copies of data an app keeps to open faster next time. If System Data keeps growing after a restart, clear large files from inside the apps that made them, not from the Finder.</p>
<h2>Myths about freeing space on a Mac you can ignore</h2>
<p>One popular belief is that you need a cleaner app. Downloadable cleaners promise to find junk your Mac cannot, but macOS already clears temporary files on its own. Some cleaners delete files an app still needs. The Storage screen and the Finder do the same job with tools Apple built.</p>
<p>Another myth is that deleting caches frees space for good. Caches rebuild the next time you open the app, so the space comes back within days. Clearing caches helps in one narrow case: an app that misbehaves because its cache is damaged. That is a fix for a broken app, not a way to free disk space.</p>
<p>Third is the belief that a full disk is always why a Mac feels slow. Nearly full disks do cause real trouble: updates need room to download, and apps need space for temporary files. But slowness has many causes. Free the space for the update or the project that needs it, and judge speed separately.</p>
<p>Last comes the mistake of treating the Trash as a second storage shelf. Files in the Trash still occupy disk space. Apple is blunt about it: storage is not freed until you empty the Trash. Files you might need again belong in a folder, not in the Trash. Check the Trash before you empty it, because emptying is permanent.</p>
<ul><li>Backed up: files you are about to delete exist somewhere else, or you accept losing them.</li><li>Trash checked: you looked inside before emptying, because emptying is permanent.</li><li>Library untouched: nothing inside ~/Library was deleted by hand.</li><li>Space confirmed: the free-space figure rose after you emptied the Trash.</li></ul>
<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>How much free space should a Mac have?</h3>
<p>There is no fixed number Apple publishes. Leave enough room for the next macOS update plus the largest file you work with. With almost no free space, a Mac cannot download updates or save large files.</p>
<h3>Does deleting apps free up disk space on a Mac?</h3>
<p>Yes, once you empty the Trash. Deleting an app moves it to the Trash, and the space returns only when the Trash is emptied. Large apps such as games and video editors free the most space.</p>
<h3>Is it safe to delete System Data on a Mac?</h3>
<p>There is no single System Data file to delete, because it is a category, not a folder. Restart the Mac first, since temporary files clear on shutdown. Never delete files from the Library folder by hand.</p>
<h3>Will Store in iCloud delete my files?</h3>
<p>No. Store in iCloud moves copies of your Desktop and Documents files to iCloud Drive. It keeps the ones you opened recently on the Mac. Anything kept only in iCloud downloads again when you open it.</p>
<h3>Why is my Mac still full after I deleted files?</h3>
<p>Most of the time, the reason is the Trash. Deleted files keep using space until you empty it. Look inside the Trash first, check nothing there is needed, and choose Finder &gt; Empty Trash.</p>
