<h2>What is 6G and when is it coming?</h2>
<p>6G is the sixth generation of mobile phone networks, and the first networks are still years away. No carrier sells a 6G plan, and no phone you can buy connects to a 6G network. The short answer is that industry roadmaps point to around 2030, with the first commercial networks expected near the end of the decade. That date is a target, not a promise, and it could slip by a year or two. This guide explains what it is, what it could do, and what to do now.</p>
<p>Analogue voice calls arrived in the 1980s, and digital calls and texts followed in the 1990s. Third generation networks in the 2000s brought the mobile internet. Fourth generation networks in the 2010s made video on a phone normal. Fifth generation networks, from 2019 onwards, added faster speeds and the low delay that factories started to use. Sixth generation networks aim to add sensing and built-in artificial intelligence to that list.</p>
<p>Think of 6G as a network that notices the world around it, not just a faster pipe for data. Today&#x27;s networks move data between your phone and a mast. A 6G network could also sense objects, people and movement, a little like radar, using the same radio waves. That idea has a name: integrated sensing and communication. It is one of several uses the ITU describes in its IMT-2030 framework for 6G. The framework also lists AI built into the network itself, so it can manage itself.</p>

<h2>How 6G differs from 5G</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse4.mm.bing.net/th?q=Two+phones+side+by+side%2C+one+showing+5G+and+one+showing+a+future+network&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="Two phones side by side, one showing 5G and one showing a future network" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>5G is the network most new phones use today. It already gives fast downloads, low delay and room for many devices on one mast. 6G keeps all of that and pushes three things further: speed, delay and awareness. The ITU framework targets peak speeds around one terabit per second, far above the headline peak of 5G. A terabit is a thousand gigabits. Real speeds will be far lower. Higher frequencies also travel less far and pass through walls poorly, so more masts are needed.</p>
<p>Delay matters as much as speed. Delay is the time between tapping something and the network responding, and engineers call it latency. 5G cut latency to a few thousandths of a second in good conditions. 6G aims to cut it further, toward a tenth of that, so a remote control or a headset feels instant. Coverage is the third change. 6G plans to reach places today&#x27;s masts do not. Satellites and high platforms will work alongside ground masts, so a signal follows you into a valley or out to sea. That part is called ubiquitous connectivity, which means a connection almost everywhere.</p>
<p>Speed is not the whole story. A 6G mast could detect a car crossing a junction or a person falling at home, and warn someone in time. Phones could sense gestures without a camera, so a wave of the hand works in the dark. None of this is certain yet. What the industry agrees on is that 6G is a network that senses and thinks, while 5G is mostly a network that carries.</p>
<h2>The road to 2030: who decides what 6G is</h2>
<p>No single company invents 6G. Two bodies shape it, and they do different jobs. The ITU, the International Telecommunication Union, sets the global vision and the goals a network must meet to earn the 6G name. Its IMT-2030 framework, published in late 2023 as Recommendation M.2160, describes several uses and sets targets for speed, delay and device numbers. 3GPP, the group that wrote the 5G standard, turns those goals into the detailed rules phone makers and carriers build to.</p>
<p>That work runs in stages called Releases. 3GPP&#x27;s work proceeds in study and specification phases. Engineers first test candidate ideas and write reports, then write the rules a network can actually be built to. According to 3GPP&#x27;s published schedule, the study phase runs through the mid-2020s and the first buildable rules follow in the late 2020s. Spectrum, the radio frequencies a network uses, is decided in parallel. According to the ITU&#x27;s published agenda, its World Radiocommunication Conference in 2027, known as WRC-27, will consider which new bands 6G can use.</p>
<p>After the rules come the trials. Carriers and makers will test 6G in small areas, then larger ones. Industry roadmaps point to early commercial networks around 2030, with wider rollout in the early 2030s. Every generation before it took about ten years from first vision to first customers. If any stage slips, and standards work often slips by a year, the honest date is the early 2030s, not tomorrow.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Late 2023: the ITU publishes the IMT-2030 vision for 6G.</li><li>Mid-2020s: 3GPP studies 6G ideas and writes reports.</li><li>2027: the ITU&#x27;s WRC-27 conference considers new radio frequencies.</li><li>Late 2020s: 3GPP writes the first buildable rules.</li><li>Around 2030: industry roadmaps point to the first commercial networks.</li></ol>
<h2>What 6G could actually do for you</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse1.mm.bing.net/th?q=Person+using+a+phone+on+a+train+with+a+city+passing+outside+the+window&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="Person using a phone on a train with a city passing outside the window" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>For most people, the first change will be invisible: the same apps, running a little faster, with less waiting. Big downloads, such as game updates or offline maps, could finish in seconds. Video calls could stay sharp on a moving train. It needs a new phone, a new plan and a mast near you, all of which arrive after 2030.</p>
<p>Daily life could feel different in another way. A 6G network could spot a car approaching a blind corner and warn a driver before they see it. It could notice an older person who has fallen at home and alert a family member. A headset could track your hands by radio waves alone, so virtual objects stay put when you move. These uses are research projects today, not products, and each raises privacy questions regulators have not answered yet.</p>
<p>Coverage is the other promise. Satellites and high platforms could fill the gaps where masts do not reach. A hiker, a farmer or a ship at sea could keep a connection. It also helps small sensors in farms, factories and cities that report soil, traffic or power use. 6G is built for those devices as much as for phones, with room for far more of them than 5G allows.</p>
<h2>What to do now: wait and keep your 5G phone</h2>
<p>Nothing you buy today will connect to 6G, so spend nothing on it yet. Some phones and accessories already carry a 6G-ready label, and that label means nothing. There is no 6G network to be ready for, and no standard to be ready with. A phone bought now will be old before 6G arrives. Choose your next phone for its camera, battery and price, not for a future network.</p>
<p>If your contract is up for renewal, 5G is the right choice. It is fast, widely available and still improving. A 5G phone bought today will serve you until 6G phones exist. The first 6G plans will be expensive and patchy, as 5G plans were in 2019. Wait for the second wave, when prices fall and coverage grows.</p>
<p>Home broadband stays as it is too. 6G will not replace the Wi-Fi router in your living room, because radio spectrum is shared and slows down when everyone uses it at once. A cable into your home does not slow down that way. Keep your broadband and your Wi-Fi. Let 6G be a network for when you are out and about, not a reason to change anything at home.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Ignore any 6G-ready label on a phone or accessory.</li><li>Choose your next phone for battery, camera and price.</li><li>Renew on 5G, and let it serve you until 2030.</li><li>Check coverage again near 2030, when the first networks go live.</li></ol>
<h2>Myths you can ignore about 6G</h2>
<p>Some people say 6G is already here. Check for a coverage map: no carrier has published one, because the standard is still being written. Lab tests and press releases are not a network. A real 6G launch needs a finished standard, agreed spectrum and phones that support it, and none of those exist yet. Until carriers announce a launch date with a coverage map, treat every 6G claim as marketing.</p>
<p>Others say 6G will replace Wi-Fi and home broadband. It will not, at least not at home. Radio spectrum is shared, so a mast slows down when everyone uses it at once, while a cable into your home does not. Wi-Fi stays cheaper and faster indoors, and 6G is built for movement, not for replacing the router in your living room.</p>
<p>A third myth says you need to prepare for 6G now. You do not. There is no setting to change, no app to install and no plan to buy. When 6G arrives, your carrier will tell you. Until then, the best preparation is a phone with a good battery and a plan that fits your budget.</p>
<ul><li>Myth: 6G is here. Fact: the standard is still being written.</li><li>Myth: 6G replaces Wi-Fi. Fact: Wi-Fi stays faster and cheaper at home.</li><li>Myth: you must prepare now. Fact: there is nothing to do until 2030.</li><li>Myth: a 6G-ready label matters. Fact: the label is marketing, not a feature.</li></ul>
<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Will my 5G phone work on 6G?</h3>
<p>No, it cannot. 6G needs new radio hardware in the phone, so a 5G phone cannot connect to a 6G network. Your 5G phone will keep working on 5G and 4G, which will run alongside 6G for years after launch.</p>
<h3>When can I buy a 6G phone?</h3>
<p>Not before the first networks go live, expected around 2030. The first 6G phones will arrive with them, and they will be expensive at first. Most people should wait for the second wave, when prices fall and coverage grows.</p>
<h3>Will 6G cost more than 5G?</h3>
<p>No 6G prices exist yet, so nobody can say. New generations usually start expensive and fall in price as coverage grows, which is what happened with 5G. Judge a plan when you can see its price and coverage map.</p>
<h3>Do I need 6G for my apps?</h3>
<p>No, you do not. Streaming, video calls, maps and games all run well on 4G and 5G today. 6G adds speed and sensing for new uses, such as headsets and self-driving cars, but it does not change what your current apps need.</p>
<h3>What does IMT-2030 mean?</h3>
<p>IMT-2030 is the ITU&#x27;s name for the 6G vision. IMT stands for International Mobile Telecommunications, and 2030 is the target year. The framework lists six uses and the targets a network must meet to be called 6G.</p>
