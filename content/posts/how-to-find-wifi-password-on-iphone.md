<h2>Why your iPhone already knows the password</h2>
<p>Your iPhone saves the key for networks you joined with a standard password. You typed it once. The phone stored it for next time. Hotel sign-in pages and work networks may store no key you can reveal.</p>
<p>A Wi-Fi password is the key that lets a device join a locked network. The router checks that key before it allows any traffic through. Wrong key, no connection. Right key, full access.</p>
<p>Saved means your phone joined that network before. A network you never joined has no saved key to show. That limit shapes every method below.</p>
<p>Apple keeps these keys behind your lock screen check. Your face, finger or passcode proves the phone is yours. Anyone holding the phone still faces that check. The extra tap protects every saved network.</p>
<p>One common belief says you must reset the router to see the password. That belief is wrong for any network your phone already joined. Check the phone first. Router resets come last, not first.</p>
<p>Guests ask for this password more than any other detail. A saved key answers that request in seconds. You stay in control of who gets access. Nobody needs to reset anything.</p>
<div class="note"><b>Check this:</b> Check Settings &gt; Wi-Fi first. If the network shows a check mark, the password is already saved.</div>

<h2>How to find wifi password on iphone for the network you use now</h2>
<p>Start with the network you use right now, because that path is the shortest route to the saved password. The Settings route works on iOS 16 or later. Follow the labels your screen shows.</p>
<p>Open the Settings app and choose Wi-Fi near the top of the list, where it sits beside Bluetooth and other connection controls on most iPhones. Find the network with the blue check mark beside its name. That check means your phone uses it now. Press the small info button on the right.</p>
<p>Tap the Password field on the next screen. Your phone asks you to prove the phone is yours. Pass that check and the password appears in plain text. Tap Copy if you need it elsewhere.</p>
<p>Read the password with care before you type it anywhere, because Wi-Fi passwords are case-sensitive and one wrong capital letter can block the join completely. Copy and paste beats typing by hand. That simple habit saves retries.</p>
<p>Close the screen when you finish. The password hides again on its own. Nobody needs to see it over your shoulder. That habit keeps guests honest. Small care, big payoff.</p>
<p>Screenshots of this screen carry the full password. Keep those images out of shared albums and group chats. One careless share hands out your network key. Treat the screen like a house key.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Open Settings and tap Wi-Fi.</li><li>Tap the info button next to the network with the blue check mark.</li><li>Tap the Password field.</li><li>Pass the Face ID, Touch ID or passcode check to reveal the password.</li><li>Tap Copy to copy the password for use elsewhere.</li></ol>
<h2>Old networks you joined before sit under Edit</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse3.mm.bing.net/th?q=saved+networks+list+on+iphone&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="saved networks list on iphone" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>Old networks hide under one extra tap, and a hotel network from last year or a friend&#x27;s house from last month still counts as saved. Your phone kept those keys too.</p>
<p>Open Settings and choose Wi-Fi. Use Edit in the upper-right corner to show the full list of networks your phone joined before. This Edit list needs iOS 16 or later. Your phone then reveals networks it joined before.</p>
<p>Find the network you want in that list. Tap the info button beside its name. Tap the Password field and confirm the check again. The saved password appears for that network.</p>
<p>Networks with a lock icon need a password to join. Open networks have no password to reveal. Your list may mix both kinds. Pick the locked one you need.</p>
<p>Forget a network only when you mean it. Forgetting deletes the saved key from your phone. You would need the password again to rejoin. Leave old entries alone unless space or safety demands action.</p>
<p>Deleting old entries cleans the list but removes access. Keep entries for places you visit again. Remove entries for places you will never revisit. Small lists stay easy to search.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Go to Settings, then choose Wi-Fi from the list.</li><li>Tap Edit in the upper-right corner and pass the identity check when asked.</li><li>Tap the info button next to the saved network you want.</li><li>Choose the Password row and confirm the check to see the saved key.</li></ol>
<h2>The Passwords app puts every saved network in one list</h2>
<p>iOS 18 adds a Passwords app for saved logins. Wi-Fi networks live there beside website logins and other saved credentials. One list beats hunting through settings.</p>
<p>Open the Passwords app and prove the phone is yours. Tap Wi-Fi to see saved networks. The network you use now sits near the top.</p>
<p>Tap the network name you need, then choose the Password field to reveal the password and copy it from that same screen. Search helps when the list runs long.</p>
<p>Pick the route you remember best. Settings suits the network in use now. The app suits browsing older entries by name.</p>
<p>Keep the app locked down like any password store. Your phone lock is the first wall. The app check is the second wall. Two walls beat one every time. Guard both.</p>
<p>Search saves time when dozens of networks pile up. Type part of the network name to narrow the list. Exact spelling helps when names look alike. Careful taps prevent wrong passwords.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Open the Passwords app and pass the identity check.</li><li>Tap Wi-Fi to see your saved networks.</li><li>Pick the network you want, then choose the Password row to reveal its key.</li></ol>

<h2>Sharing beats reading the password aloud</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse4.mm.bing.net/th?q=two+iphones+sharing+wifi+password&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="two iphones sharing wifi password" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>Reading a password aloud invites mistakes, because long passwords with mixed capitals, numbers and symbols break easily when spoken across a noisy room. Sharing sends the key without spelling it out. Apple built two sharing routes for this job.</p>
<p>The pop-up route works between nearby Apple devices. Both people keep each other saved in Contacts. Both devices keep Wi-Fi and Bluetooth switched on. Proximity matters here.</p>
<p>Ask the other person to choose your network name first. Their device pauses at the password screen. Your iPhone shows its Wi-Fi Password prompt. Tap Share Password, then tap Done.</p>
<p>A QR code route helps guests without Apple devices. Open the Passwords app and tap Wi-Fi. Tap your network, then tap Show Network QR Code. Your guest points their camera at the code and joins.</p>
<p>Show the code on your screen in person. Do not send screenshots around. A screenshot left in a chat thread keeps working long after the visit ends.</p>
<p>Guests with Android phones can scan the same QR code. Their camera or Wi-Fi settings handles the join. No typing means no spelling errors. Everyone connects on the first try.</p>
<div class="note warn"><b>Warning:</b> A photo of the QR code works like the password. Show it in person and never post it.</div>
<h2>When the password is not on your phone at all</h2>
<p>Some passwords never reach your phone, because a network you never joined leaves no saved key behind and a forgotten network deletes its key too. Other sources must fill the gap.</p>
<p>Check the router label before you call anyone. Many routers print the network name and default password on a sticker underneath. That default works only if nobody changed it. Changed passwords live elsewhere.</p>
<p>Ask the person who runs the network, since a host, landlord or office manager set that password and can share it or type it for you. Guessing wastes everyone&#x27;s time.</p>
<p>Your internet provider may show the password in its app. Provider apps differ by company and country, and Wi-Fi controls can hide under different labels. Follow the labels inside your own app. Router admin pages also show or change the password.</p>
<p>A Mac signed in to the same Apple Account can help. Open the Passwords app on the Mac and click Wi-Fi. Choose the network name to show or copy its key.</p>
<p>Resetting the router stays the last resort here. A reset changes the password for every device at home. Each phone, laptop and TV then needs the new key. Try every saved route before that step.</p>
<ul><li>Router label: check under the router for the printed network name and default password.</li><li>Network owner: ask the host or manager who set the password up.</li><li>Provider app: look inside your internet provider app for Wi-Fi settings.</li><li>Your Mac: open Passwords, click Wi-Fi, and select the network.</li></ul>
<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Can my iPhone show a password for a network I never joined?</h3>
<p>No. Your iPhone stores passwords only for networks it joined before. A new network has no saved key to reveal. Ask the network owner for the password instead.</p>
<h3>Why does my phone ask for Face ID before showing the password?</h3>
<p>The check proves the phone is in your hands. Saved Wi-Fi passwords open your home network to anyone who holds them. Your face, finger or passcode gates that access.</p>
<h3>The Share Password pop-up never appears. What should I check?</h3>
<p>Check Contacts first. Each person needs the other person&#x27;s Apple Account email or phone number saved. Turn Wi-Fi and Bluetooth on for both devices. Turn Personal Hotspot off and keep the devices close.</p>
<h3>Can I see the same password on my Mac?</h3>
<p>Yes, when the Mac uses the same Apple Account. Open the Passwords app on the Mac and click Wi-Fi in the sidebar. Select the network to reveal or copy its password.</p>
