<h2>How do humanoid robots work?</h2>
<p>At heart, a humanoid robot is a machine shaped like a person. It has two legs, two arms, a torso and a head, and it is built to work in spaces made for people. Doors, stairs, shelves and tools are all sized for human bodies, so a robot with the same shape can use them too. That shape is the hard part. Walking on two legs is something human babies take a year or more to learn. Robots need the same care: sensors, motors and software working together in a constant loop.</p>
<p>These parts do the work, and each one needs the others. Sensors tell the robot where its body is and what is around it. Motors in the joints move the arms and legs. A computer brain turns sensor readings into motor commands. Hands grip and carry. A battery powers it all.</p>
<p>The robots you read about have names worth knowing. Boston Dynamics builds Atlas, a research robot that can run, jump and pick itself up after a fall. Companies such as Agility Robotics, Figure and Tesla also develop humanoid robots. Some have been trialled in warehouses or factories under controlled programs. None is widely sold to consumers as a home appliance.</p>

<h2>The five systems inside every humanoid</h2>
<p>Every humanoid robot is built from the same parts. Perception is the senses: cameras, laser scanners and touch sensors that map the world. Locomotion is the legs and the balance software that keeps the robot upright. Manipulation is the arms and hands that pick things up. The brain is the computer that plans each move. Power is the battery and the wiring that feeds the motors. Each one answers a question: where am I, where am I going, what am I holding, what next, and do I have the energy?</p>
<p>These systems talk to each other constantly. Cameras spot a box, the brain plans a path, the legs walk there, and the hands grip it with just enough force. That chain runs as a loop: sense, plan, move, sense again. If a foot slips, the sensors report it at once, and the brain shifts the robot&#x27;s weight before it falls. Control loops run at different rates. Low-level motor controllers and state estimators often run at hundreds to a few thousand cycles a second, while the planning loop runs slower. A walking robot needs a fast computer on board, not a remote one. A delay of even a tenth of a second can mean a fall.</p>
<p>Power sets the limits. Motors are hungry, and a humanoid carries its battery with it, so weight matters twice. A bigger battery lasts longer but makes the robot heavier, and a heavier robot needs stronger motors. Battery life varies greatly. Small research humanoids may last tens of minutes under continuous walking, while industrial designs built for long shifts can run several hours. Exact endurance depends on the motors, the weight and the job. Some swap batteries to keep going. That trade-off is why humanoids are still rare.</p>
<h2>How a robot walks without falling over</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse3.mm.bing.net/th?q=Humanoid+robot+taking+a+step%2C+one+foot+raised+off+the+floor&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="Humanoid robot taking a step, one foot raised off the floor" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>Walking is the hardest problem in humanoid robotics, and balance is the reason. Engineers track a point called the centre of mass: the average position of all the robot&#x27;s weight. As long as that point stays over the area covered by the feet on the ground, the robot stays upright. Lift one foot, and that area shrinks to a single shoe, so the robot must shift its weight first.</p>
<p>Engineers use a second idea called the zero moment point, or ZMP. It is the point on the ground where the robot&#x27;s weight and movement balance out. As long as the ZMP stays inside the shape traced by the feet on the ground, the robot will not tip. The robot&#x27;s brain watches the ZMP constantly. It uses data from its inner-ear sensor and the force sensors in its feet, and moves the hips, knees and ankles to keep it inside. A push from the side moves the ZMP, so the robot steps sideways to catch itself, just as you would.</p>
<p>Each step follows the same pattern. One foot stays planted while the other swings forward, the knee bends to clear the floor, and the heel lands first. The brain plans each footstep before the foot leaves the ground, choosing where to land. Stairs, slopes and slippery floors are harder, because each landing is less certain. That is why videos of robots on flat warehouse floors look smooth. The same robots stumble on rough ground, because the floor is part of the system.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Sensors report the body&#x27;s tilt, speed and foot pressure.</li><li>The brain works out where the weight is and where it is heading.</li><li>It plans the next footstep and shifts the weight onto the standing leg.</li><li>Motors swing the other leg forward and place the foot.</li><li>The loop repeats many times a second.</li></ol>
<h2>How a robot sees, feels and picks things up</h2>
<p>A robot sees with cameras and a laser scanner called LiDAR. Cameras give colour and detail, so the robot can tell a box from a bag. LiDAR units measure distance by timing laser pulses, building a three-dimensional map of the room. Resolution, range and update rate depend on the sensor design. Together they answer two questions: what is that, and how far away is it? The map updates constantly as the robot moves, so a person walking past appears at once.</p>
<p>Feeling matters as much as seeing. An inner-ear sensor, called an IMU, reports which way is down and how fast the body is turning. Joint encoders report the exact angle of every joint, so the brain always knows where each limb is. Force sensors in the feet report how hard the ground pushes back, and force sensors in the hands report how hard the robot is gripping. Without them, a robot picking up an egg would crush it or drop it.</p>
<p>Hands are the newest part to work well. Early robots had simple grippers, like two-pronged claws, that could lift a box but not much else. Newer hands have fingers with joints and touch sensors, so they can pinch, hold and turn objects. Tesla&#x27;s Optimus hand, for example, has fingers that bend like a person&#x27;s, and Figure&#x27;s robots use hands built for warehouse boxes. Fingers still lag behind human hands: they are slower, weaker and worse at feeling texture. That gap is why most humanoid jobs today are carrying and placing, not fine work like threading a needle.</p>
<h2>What humanoid robots do today, and what they cannot do yet</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse2.mm.bing.net/th?q=Humanoid+robot+moving+boxes+on+a+shelf+in+a+warehouse&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="Humanoid robot moving boxes on a shelf in a warehouse" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>The jobs humanoids do today are in warehouses and factories. Agility&#x27;s Digit has been trialled moving boxes and totes in warehouses, walking between shelves and placing items on conveyors. Figure&#x27;s robots have tested box handling in car factories. Tesla&#x27;s Optimus has been shown sorting parts in factories. These jobs share three traits: flat floors, known objects and no surprises. That is where humanoids earn their keep today: dull, heavy, repeated lifting.</p>
<p>What they cannot do yet is almost everything else. Stairs, mud, ice and cluttered rooms still beat them. A humanoid cannot cook, clean a bathroom or fold laundry, because those jobs need soft touch, judgement and a home that changes every day. They are also expensive, need charging often, and need engineers nearby. For now, a wheeled robot or a person does most jobs better and cheaper.</p>
<p>Progress is fast, but the gap is wide. Every year brings steadier walking, better hands and longer battery life. Still, the step from a tidy warehouse to a messy home is the biggest one, and nobody has taken it yet. If you see a video of a robot doing housework, check whether it is a real home or a stage set. Most are staged, with the robot following a script and people standing by.</p>
<h2>Myths you can ignore about humanoid robots</h2>
<p>One common claim is that robots think like people. They do not. A humanoid&#x27;s brain is software trained on examples: walking data and human demonstrations. It recognises patterns and follows rules. It does not understand what it is doing, and it cannot reason about a situation it has never seen. When a robot does something clever, it is recalling a pattern, not having an idea.</p>
<p>Another claim is that robots will take every job next year. They will not, because most jobs need hands, judgement and a changing environment, and humanoids are still bad at all three. In warehouses, robots work alongside people, doing the heaviest lifting while people do the thinking. The jobs most at risk are the dull, repeated ones.</p>
<p>A third claim is that a robot that falls is broken or dangerous. Falls are normal in testing, and robots are built to take them. Boston Dynamics&#x27; Atlas picks itself up after a fall, and engineers expect falls while they teach a robot new ground. A falling robot is a risk to anything nearby. The fall itself is part of learning, not a failure.</p>
<ul><li>Myth: robots think like people. Fact: they follow patterns learned from training data.</li><li>Myth: robots will take every job. Fact: they handle dull, repeated lifting in warehouses.</li><li>Myth: a fall means the robot failed. Fact: falls are part of testing and teaching.</li><li>Myth: humanoids will clean your home soon. Fact: homes are far harder than warehouses.</li></ul>
<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>How many joints does a humanoid robot have?</h3>
<p>Dozens. Atlas has several dozen actuated joints, and most humanoids have dozens. Each joint has a motor and a sensor, so the brain knows where every limb is.</p>
<h3>How do humanoid robots keep their balance?</h3>
<p>They track their centre of mass and the zero moment point, and keep both over the feet on the ground. Sensors report tilt and foot pressure constantly, and the brain shifts weight and steps to catch itself.</p>
<h3>Can a humanoid robot climb stairs?</h3>
<p>Some can, slowly, on even stairs. Each step changes the balance, and a missed step means a fall. Flat floors are where they work best today.</p>
<h3>How long does a humanoid robot&#x27;s battery last?</h3>
<p>It varies greatly by robot and job. Small research robots may last tens of minutes of continuous walking, while industrial designs can run several hours. Some swap batteries so they can keep working while one charges.</p>
<h3>Will humanoid robots work in homes?</h3>
<p>Not soon. Homes are cluttered and uneven, and robots are still bad at soft touch and judgement.</p>
