<div class="key"><b>In short:</b> Wired charging delivers more power to the battery than wireless charging, because a cable connects directly while a pad loses energy in two coil conversions and as heat. Compare the two yourself by charging for the same twenty minutes from the same starting percentage.<ul><li>This article uses delivered power to mean energy measured at the phone or battery, not the wall outlet.</li><li>Wireless charging usually loses more energy as heat because it uses two coil conversions.</li><li>USB Power Delivery negotiates the voltage and current your phone can accept, so a high-rated charger may deliver less.</li><li>Alignment, cases, temperature and battery level can reduce wireless charging speed.</li><li>For overnight charging, switch on your phone’s charge limit where one is offered.</li></ul></div>
<h2>A rated wattage is not a battery reading</h2>
<p>Wattage measures the rate at which electrical energy moves. It equals volts multiplied by amps.</p>
<p>The wattage on the box is a maximum the charger can supply. How much your phone takes from it changes as the battery fills.</p>
<p>Power passes through the wall adapter, cable, charging chip and battery. Each stage uses some energy. Wireless charging adds a transmitter coil and a receiving coil, with a magnetic link between them.</p>

<h2>Wired charging usually sends more power to the phone</h2>
<p>A cable usually wastes less energy than a pad. A cable makes a direct electrical connection, while a pad converts electricity into a magnetic field and then back again.</p>
<p>A phone at 10% charge often accepts far more power than the same phone at 90%, and a thick case can slow a pad that otherwise works well. Public test results also vary because reviewers measure at different points.</p>
<p>This article avoids presenting one efficiency range as a universal result. A wall-meter reading includes the adapter and its own losses. A USB meter measures power between the adapter and phone.</p>
<div class="note"><b>Check this:</b> Compare chargers by battery gained over the same time, not by the number on the box.</div>
<h2>USB Power Delivery changes the number your phone accepts</h2>
<p>USB Power Delivery, or USB PD, lets a charger and phone negotiate a power mode. The adapter advertises supported voltage and current combinations. The phone requests one it can use.</p>
<p>A 30W USB PD charger may offer 5V, 9V, 15V or other profiles. Your phone might request only a lower profile, especially when its battery is warm or nearly full.</p>
<p>The cable matters too. Some USB-C cables support lower current than others. A suitable charger, cable and phone must agree before higher power becomes available.</p>
<p>Fast charging also changes over time. Phones often accept more power at a low battery level, then reduce it as the battery fills. That protects the battery and controls heat.</p>

<h2>Wireless charging loses power through coils and heat</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse4.mm.bing.net/th?q=Phone+charging+on+a+wireless+pad+with+a+temperature+reading+nearby&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="Phone charging on a wireless pad with a temperature reading nearby" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>Wireless charging sends energy across a short gap. The pad’s coil creates a magnetic field, and the phone’s coil turns that field back into electrical power.</p>
<p>Both conversions use energy. Poor alignment, extra distance and foreign materials can increase losses. The pad and phone can then become warmer while the battery charges more slowly.</p>
<p>Phones commonly reduce wireless power when the charging area warms. The exact limit varies by model, software, pad and temperature.</p>
<p>A misaligned coil can substantially reduce delivered power. The drop may be small on one phone and severe on another, so a claim such as 15W falling to 5W needs a specific test.</p>
<h2>Heat matters more than the charging label</h2>
<p>Lithium-ion batteries age through charge cycles, time spent at high charge and temperature. Heat combined with a full battery can speed capacity loss.</p>
<p>The charging method does not create a separate kind of battery cycle. A cycle reflects the amount of energy used and replaced, whether it arrived through a cable or a coil.</p>
<p>Wireless charging can create more warmth in some setups. Keep the phone off hot surfaces, remove unsuitable cases and use the manufacturer’s battery-protection feature when possible.</p>
<p>Apple calls its feature Optimised Battery Charging. Samsung offers Battery protection settings, and other Android phones use names such as Adaptive Charging.</p>
<div class="note"><b>Tip:</b> Apple: Settings &gt; Battery &gt; Battery Health &amp; Charging &gt; Optimised Battery Charging. Samsung: Settings &gt; Battery &gt; Battery protection. Menu names can differ by software version.</div>
<h2>Alignment and cases can slow a wireless pad</h2>
<p>Place the phone’s receiving coil over the centre of the pad. Small alignment errors can reduce delivered power, especially on flat pads without magnets.</p>
<p>Cases add distance between the coils. MagSafe and Qi2 accessories are specified to work through cases up to roughly 2 mm, and thicker or metal-containing cases may reduce delivered power. Metal plates, cards and some magnetic accessories can interrupt charging.</p>
<p>Qi2 and MagSafe-style magnets help keep the coils aligned. They improve consistency, not the phone’s maximum charging rate. The phone, case and pad still need compatible hardware.</p>
<p>An iPhone X, for example, supports Qi wireless charging but not newer magnetic alignment features built into later accessories. A Samsung Galaxy S-series phone may support faster wireless modes only with a compatible Samsung pad and the right conditions. Check the exact model specification before comparing chargers.</p>
<ul><li>Centre the phone on a flat pad before judging its speed.</li><li>Remove cards, metal plates and unsuitable magnetic accessories.</li><li>Test with and without the case if charging seems slow.</li><li>Check whether the phone and pad support the same charging standard.</li><li>Use a Qi2 or MagSafe-compatible setup when stable alignment matters.</li></ul>
<h2>You can test your own charger without guessing</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse3.mm.bing.net/th?q=USB-C+power+meter+connected+between+a+charger+and+smartphone&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="USB-C power meter connected between a charger and smartphone" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>Run an A/B test with the same phone, battery percentage and room temperature. Use the same time period, such as 20 minutes, and stop other demanding apps.</p>
<p>For wired charging, use a USB-C power meter between the adapter and cable if the connector supports it. The meter shows input to the phone, not necessarily power stored at the battery.</p>
<p>Some Android phones show charging current or power in diagnostic apps, but those readings are estimates and can be filtered by software. iPhones usually give battery percentage rather than a live wattage figure.</p>
<p>For wireless charging, start with the phone at the same percentage and place it carefully. Record the battery gain, surface temperature and any slow-charging message. Repeat with the case removed.</p>
<p>The cleanest comparison uses three runs for each method. Average the percentage gained, then note the conditions. Battery percentage is a rough result, not a laboratory efficiency measurement.</p>

<h2>Choose the method based on time and heat</h2>
<p>If you need a quick top-up, a cable is usually faster. A direct connection and USB PD negotiation can deliver more usable power, provided the phone and charger support it.</p>
<p>For overnight charging when speed is not critical, wireless is convenient. Let the phone stop at a set level where that choice is offered, and keep the pad away from heat.</p>
<p>A cable also suits travel, gaming and car charging when you need predictable power. Wireless works well at a desk or bedside table, where convenience matters more than the shortest charge time.</p>
<p>Neither method guarantees a fixed rate. Your phone controls the result.</p>

<div class="note warn"><b>Common mistake:</b> Do not compare a wall-meter reading with a battery estimate and call them the same measurement. The measurement point changes the result.</div>
<div class="note"><b>Our take:</b> For speed and predictable delivery, wired charging usually wins. Wireless charging earns its place through convenience, especially when you have time.</div>
<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Is wireless charging bad for battery health?</h3>
<p>Not by itself. Heat and long periods at a high charge level cause more concern. Keep the phone cool and take it off the pad once it is full.</p>
<h3>Does a higher-rated pad always charge faster?</h3>
<p>No. The phone, pad, alignment, temperature and charging protocol set the actual rate. A lower-rated wireless pad can still beat a cable on convenience, and a higher-rated pad can still lose to a cable on delivered power, because the phone sets the rate.</p>
<h3>Why does my phone slow down on a wireless pad?</h3>
<p>Heat, battery level, alignment and case materials can all reduce power. Try a different pad and a cooler spot to find the cause.</p>
<h3>How can I measure charging power?</h3>
<p>Use a USB power meter for wired input, or compare battery gain over equal time for wireless charging. Neither method directly proves power at the battery terminals.</p>
<h3>Can I leave my phone on a wireless pad overnight?</h3>
<p>Usually, yes. A pad suits slow overnight charging; keep the phone and pad away from heat.</p>
