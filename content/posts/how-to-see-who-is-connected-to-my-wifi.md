<h2>How to see who is connected to my Wi-Fi</h2>
<p>Every phone, laptop, TV and smart speaker that joins your Wi-Fi asks the router for a connection, and the router writes each one down. That list is the answer, and you just need to know where your router keeps it.</p>
<p>Most routers now come with a phone app, and each maker names the list differently. In the Netgear Nighthawk app, the connected-device list sits under a section named something like Devices or Device Manager, depending on app version. Google shows it under Devices in the Google Home app. TP-Link puts it under Clients in the Tether app. ASUS calls its list Clients, though the name can vary by app version. Eero calls it Devices. Open the app your router maker provides, sign in, and tap that list. If your internet provider supplied the router, look in your provider&#x27;s own app instead: the list often lives there, not in a maker page.</p>
<p>Each device appears by name when the router knows it. You will see entries like a phone with its owner&#x27;s name or a living-room TV. Some show only a brand, and a few show only a code instead of a name. Those codes are normal, and later sections explain what they mean.</p>
<p>Count the entries and compare them with your home, because that count often surprises people. Smart gadgets add up fast, so count your phones, laptops, TVs and smart plugs first. A much longer list suggests a stranger.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Open your router maker&#x27;s app on your phone.</li><li>Sign in with your router account or admin password.</li><li>Tap Devices, Clients or Attached Devices.</li><li>Scroll the list and note anything you do not recognise.</li></ol>

<h2>Check the list on your router&#x27;s web page instead</h2>
<p>No app? Use a browser instead, since any laptop or phone on your Wi-Fi can open the router&#x27;s settings page. Type the router&#x27;s address into the address bar: common addresses are routerlogin.net for Netgear and tplinkwifi.net for TP-Link. Many routers also answer at 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.0.1, and your router&#x27;s label or manual states its own address.</p>
<p>Sign in with the admin password, which is not your Wi-Fi password. Look for it on a sticker on the router. Some older Netgear models shipped with admin as the user name and password as the password. If your router is old enough to accept those, change the admin password today. Anyone on your network could otherwise open this page.</p>
<p>Now find the device list: Netgear calls it Attached Devices on the basic home screen. TP-Link shows a Network Map on the Basic tab, and a fuller list under Advanced, then Network, then DHCP Client List. Other makers use names like Connected Devices or Client List. Wording varies by brand, so look for devices, clients or network map.</p>
<p>Wired and wireless devices appear in separate lists: wired means plugged in with a cable, while wireless means connected over Wi-Fi. Each row shows a name, an IP address and a MAC address. An IP address is the number the router lent the device, and its MAC address is the device&#x27;s fixed hardware code. Refresh the page to update the list.</p>
<p>If the label is gone, find the address on your phone. On iPhone, open Settings, then Wi-Fi, tap the network name, and read the Router address. On Android, look for Gateway in the network details. Type that number into your browser.</p>
<p>If no password works at all, a factory reset restores the defaults. Hold the small reset button with a paperclip until the lights change, then wait for the restart. That erases your Wi-Fi name and password too, so you will set the router up again.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Connect your laptop or phone to your Wi-Fi.</li><li>Type your router&#x27;s address into a browser.</li><li>Sign in with the admin password from the router label.</li><li>Open Attached Devices, Clients or DHCP Client List.</li></ol>
<h2>What the names and addresses on the list mean</h2>
<p>Three details identify each device, and of the three, the name is the friendliest. Phones and laptops often send their own names. An IP address is a local number like 192.168.1.14 that only works inside your home. Your router hands it out when a device joins, and it can change later. A MAC address is a longer code of letters and numbers that stays with the device&#x27;s Wi-Fi chip.</p>
<p>Some entries look strange, and most are harmless. Some devices show as Unknown because they never sent a name, while smart bulbs, plugs and cameras often hide behind brand names or codes. One printer may appear twice, once for Wi-Fi and once for a cable. Two entries for one gadget are normal.</p>
<p>Phones now disguise themselves on purpose: iPhones and Android phones can use a private address that changes per network. Apple calls this Private Wi-Fi Address, and it stops shops and hotspots tracking your phone. It also means the MAC address on your router list may not match the one printed on the phone box. The phone is still yours, so look at the name instead.</p>
<p>Your router list is a snapshot, not a history. The Google Home app can list devices that have connected recently, including ones now offline. Most router admin pages show only what is connected right now. Check your app&#x27;s own wording rather than assuming a fixed window. Any gadget that left yesterday will not appear on a router page. That is fine: you are looking for what is connected now, not last week.</p>

<h2>How to work out what an unknown device is</h2>
<p>Do not block a device the moment it looks unfamiliar, because it is probably yours. Work through your home first and list every gadget that uses Wi-Fi. Phones, tablets, laptops, TVs, speakers, printers, cameras, bulbs, plugs and game consoles all count. Most homes hold more than people expect, and even a forgotten smart plug can look like an intruder.</p>
<p>Match by MAC address, since every device can show its own MAC address in its settings. On Windows, open Settings, then Network and internet, then Wi-Fi, then Hardware properties, and read Physical address (MAC). On iPhone, open Settings, then Wi-Fi, tap the network name, and read the Wi-Fi Address. Compare that code with the unknown entry, because one match ends the mystery.</p>
<p>Use the switch-off test: turn a suspect gadget off, refresh the router list, and see if an entry disappears. Turn it back on and watch the entry return. This test works well for smart bulbs, speakers and cameras, and takes a minute per gadget.</p>
<p>Check how much data it used, since some router apps show how much data each device has used or when it last connected. One that used data at 3 a.m. deserves attention. Data use tells you what the gadget does, not just that it exists.</p>
<h2>What to do if a device is not yours</h2>
<p>First, block it: most router apps let you tap a device and choose Block or Pause. Netgear&#x27;s Device Manager offers Block This Device, and TP-Link&#x27;s Tether app offers a block list. It loses Wi-Fi at once, yet the stranger still knows your password.</p>
<p>Next, change your Wi-Fi password in your router&#x27;s wireless settings. Pick a long one you have not used before. Every device must reconnect, including yours. Without the new password, the stranger cannot return, so write it down somewhere safe.</p>
<p>Check the admin password too, and if you never changed it from the default, change it now. Someone on your network could open the router page and undo your block. That password guards the router itself, so keep it different from the Wi-Fi password.</p>
<p>Set up a guest network for visitors: think of it as a separate Wi-Fi name with its own password. Guests get internet but cannot see your computers or printers. Some routers also offer device isolation, which stops guests seeing each other as well as your own devices. Look for it in the guest network settings if your router offers it. Use it for visitors, babysitters and repair visits. Your main password stays private.</p>
<div class="note warn"><b>Warning:</b> Never share your Wi-Fi password by text or photo. Say it aloud or type it in yourself. A shared password spreads beyond the person you meant.</div>
<h2>Myths you can ignore about who is on your Wi-Fi</h2>
<p>One myth says a long device list means you have been hacked, but it usually means you own many gadgets. Count them before you panic: a family home with a TV, a printer and a few smart bulbs fills the list fast. Hackers are rare, while smart plugs are common.</p>
<p>Another myth says an app can name every stranger for you, but network scanner apps list the same addresses your router shows. They cannot tell you who owns a device; they only guess from the brand of the Wi-Fi chip. That guess is often wrong, so trust your router list instead.</p>
<p>People also believe changing the network name hides it, but hiding your network name, or SSID, does not hide it from scanners. It only hides it from the casual list on a phone. Only a strong password protects you, and hiding the name does not.</p>
<p>Some say you should check the list every day, but once a month is plenty for most homes. Check after guests leave or a repair visit, or if your internet suddenly slows. Regular checks beat daily worry. Set a monthly reminder.</p>
<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Can I see who is connected to my Wi-Fi from my phone?</h3>
<p>Yes. Open your router maker&#x27;s app, such as Google Home, Tether or Nighthawk, and tap Devices or Clients. No extra scanner app is needed.</p>
<h3>Can someone on my Wi-Fi see what I am doing?</h3>
<p>They can see which devices are connected and how much data each one uses. They cannot read the content of encrypted websites, since most sites today use encryption.</p>
<h3>Should I block a device I do not recognise?</h3>
<p>Identify it first with the MAC address check or the switch-off test. If it is still a stranger after that, block it and change your Wi-Fi password. Blocking without a new password only helps until the device reconnects.</p>
<h3>Does turning my Wi-Fi off and on remove strangers?</h3>
<p>No. Every device reconnects on its own when the Wi-Fi returns, including the stranger. A new password is what keeps an unwanted device out. Block the device first, then change the password.</p>
