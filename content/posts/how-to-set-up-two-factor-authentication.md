<h2>What the second step stops that a password cannot</h2>
<p>Two-factor authentication asks for two proofs at sign-in. The password proves you know a secret. The second step proves you hold a device. A thief with a leaked password has only half the lock. That gap is the whole point.</p>
<p>Password lists leak in bulk and get replayed across sites, with attackers trying the same email and password pair everywhere. A second step breaks that replay cold. The stolen password still fails at the next screen. Your account stays shut.</p>
<p>The second proof takes a few forms, from a <strong>6-digit code</strong> made by an authenticator app on your phone to a tap-to-approve prompt. A security key signs the sign-in when you touch it. Each form has a different strength.</p>
<p>Codes typed by hand can still be tricked out of you. A fake sign-in page can relay a code while it stays valid. Keys and passkeys refuse fake pages by design. That difference decides which option to pick later.</p>
<p>Start with the accounts that reset the others. Email comes first, every time. Banking, shopping and your phone account follow close behind. A social account can wait a day. Order matters more than speed here.</p>

<h2>How to set up two-factor authentication on Google</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse3.mm.bing.net/th?q=hand+scanning+QR+code+with+phone+camera&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="hand scanning QR code with phone camera" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>Google calls the feature <strong>2-Step Verification</strong>, and one switch covers Gmail, Drive, Photos and every sign-in with Google on that account. Set it up on a computer if you can. Scanning a code with your phone is easier that way. Labels vary by account, so search the page for 2-Step Verification if the wording differs.</p>
<p>Install an authenticator app before you open the settings page. Google Authenticator is the simple, free default. The app works with no signal and no mobile data. Codes come from a secret saved at setup.</p>
<p>Finish the job on the same 2-Step Verification page, where Backup codes offers a set of one-time codes for the day your phone is gone. Save the set when Google shows it.</p>
<p>Test the setup before you close the page. Sign out in a private browser window. Sign back in with password plus code. That one test proves the secret saved correctly. Skipping the test hides typos until lockout day.</p>
<p>Name the entry in the app with your full email address. Several Google accounts look alike at a glance. Clear names prevent code mix-ups later. Two minutes of naming saves real confusion.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Open your Google Account and sign in with the account you want to protect.</li><li>Search the settings page for 2-Step Verification if labels differ, then open Security and How you sign in to Google.</li><li>Choose Turn on 2-Step Verification and follow the prompts to add your first second step.</li><li>Open Authenticator in the same settings area and choose Set up authenticator.</li><li>Scan the QR code with your authenticator app, or type the setup key the page shows.</li><li>Enter the 6-digit code from the app to confirm, then save your backup codes offline.</li></ol>
<div class="note warn"><b>Warning:</b> Never read a sign-in code to a caller. Real support teams never ask for one.</div>
<h2>Turn it on for Apple and Microsoft accounts too</h2>
<p>Apple builds two-factor authentication into the Apple Account itself, with codes appearing on your trusted devices when you sign in somewhere new. A trusted phone number can also receive codes. Most iPhone owners already have it switched on. Check before you assume.</p>
<p>Microsoft uses the name two-step verification for personal accounts, and setup lives under the Security area of the account pages. Microsoft Authenticator can approve sign-ins with a tap. A passkey or security key works there too.</p>
<p>Labels shift a little between versions and account types. Work and school accounts often follow an administrator&#x27;s rules. The menu path stays close to the paths below. Follow the labels your screen actually shows.</p>
<p>A Mac follows the same Apple path in System Settings. Open your name, then Sign-In &amp; Security. The labels match the phone closely. Finish on whichever device sits in front of you. One trusted device then covers the rest.</p>
<p>Check each account once, even if you think it is on. Settings survive phone swaps in odd ways. A quick visit to each security page confirms the state. The screen shows the current state.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>On iPhone, open Settings, tap your name, then tap Sign-In &amp; Security, then Two-Factor Authentication.</li><li>Enter a trusted phone number, tap Next, then enter the verification code sent to that number.</li><li>For Microsoft, sign in to your Microsoft account and open the Security tab.</li><li>Choose Manage how I sign in, then under Two-step verification choose Turn on.</li><li>Follow the prompts and scan the QR code with your authenticator app when it appears.</li></ol>
<h3>If a menu label looks different on your screen</h3>
<p>Older screens may say Password &amp; Security instead of Sign-In &amp; Security. The control behind the label does the same job. Search the settings page for two-factor if you get stuck. Account type explains most missing options. Ask your administrator to reset your second step if policy locks you out.</p>

<h2>Which second step to pick for each account</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse2.mm.bing.net/th?q=security+key+plugged+into+laptop+side+port&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="security key plugged into laptop side port" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>Pick the strongest option each account offers you. A <strong>security key</strong> or passkey binds proof to the real site. Fake pages cannot collect a usable answer from them. That makes them the top choice where they fit.</p>
<p>An authenticator app sits one rung below keys, because codes generate on your device with no phone network involved in the process. A carrier takeover cannot redirect them. A live phishing page can still relay a typed code, though. Speed matters to that attack.</p>
<p>Text-message codes travel through your carrier to your number, so a SIM swap that moves your number to an attacker&#x27;s SIM redirects them. Codes then land in the wrong hands. That carrier step is the <strong>weak link</strong> in the chain. Use them only when nothing stronger is offered.</p>
<p>Prompts that ask you to tap yes sit between apps and texts. They remove typing, which helps. Blind tapping hurts. Read the sign-in details on every prompt. Tap no on anything you did not start.</p>
<p>Cost rarely decides this choice. Authenticator apps are free. Keys cost money once and last for years. Text codes cost nothing but depend on your carrier. Pick by strength first, then by cost.</p>

<h2>Save your way back in before you lose the phone</h2>
<p>Recovery decides whether setup helps or hurts you, because losing the phone with no backup means facing account recovery forms that take days. Those forms can fail. Ten minutes of setup now prevents that week later.</p>
<p>Backup codes are one-time passwords for emergencies. Each code works once, then dies. Store the set on paper in a drawer or safe. A photo of the codes on the same phone defeats the purpose.</p>
<p>Add a second method while the first still works, whether that means a spare key, a second authenticator, or another trusted device. Test the spare once before you rely on it. Untested backups fail at the worst moment. Google Authenticator can move codes to a new phone. On the old phone, open the menu, choose Transfer accounts, then Export accounts. Scan the code shown with the new phone.</p>
<p>Keep recovery email and phone number current as well. Old numbers get recycled to strangers. An out-of-date recovery address hands resets to whoever holds it now. Review both twice a year.</p>
<p>Photograph nothing. Screenshots of backup codes sync to cloud albums. Cloud albums sit behind the same account you are protecting. Paper in a drawer breaks that circle. Paper breaks that circle.</p>
<ul><li>Backup codes: printed, stored away from the phone</li><li>Second method: added and tested while signed in</li><li>Recovery details: current email and phone number</li><li>Authenticator transfer: moved before the old phone goes</li></ul>
<div class="note"><b>Tip:</b> Set up the new phone&#x27;s authenticator before you wipe or trade in the old one.</div>
<h2>Myths you can ignore and mistakes that lock people out</h2>
<p>One myth says two-factor authentication makes phishing impossible, yet typed codes disprove that claim daily when fake pages ask for both proofs together. The code obeys whoever asked for it. Keys exist to close that hole.</p>
<p>Another myth calls text codes pointless because swaps exist. Weak beats absent by a wide margin. Most account theft still runs on replayed passwords. A text code stops that replay cold. Turn it on, then upgrade when you can.</p>
<p>People also fear losing access more than losing the account, and that fear keeps the switch off for years on accounts that matter. Backup codes and a second method answer the fear directly. Setup with recovery saved rarely locks anyone out.</p>
<p>Approving prompts on autopilot costs the most. Attackers spam prompts until a tired tap lands. Read the location and device on each prompt. Deny anything you did not start, then change the password.</p>
<p>Habits finish what settings start. Type codes only on pages you opened yourself. Check the site name before you type anything. Slow down for ten seconds at sign-in. Hurry helps the attacker.</p>
<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>What if my phone is lost or broken?</h3>
<p>Use a backup code or your second method to sign in, then remove the lost phone from the account&#x27;s trusted devices right away. Set up the replacement phone right away. Generate fresh backup codes.</p>
<h3>Can I use one authenticator app for many accounts?</h3>
<p>Yes. Each account adds its own entry by QR code or setup key. Name each entry with the full account address. Clear names stop mix-ups when several codes sit side by side.</p>
<h3>Do authenticator codes work with no signal?</h3>
<p>Codes work offline. The app builds them from a saved secret and the device clock. No carrier, data or Wi-Fi connection takes part. If codes keep failing, set the phone clock to automatic time and try a fresh code.</p>
<h3>Should I turn this on for every account?</h3>
<p>Yes, in small batches. Begin with banking, shopping and your main phone account, then work outward. Add the rest a few at a time. Ten accounts an evening beats a perfect plan never started.</p>
