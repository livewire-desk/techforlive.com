<h2>How to transfer photos from iPhone to Android with Google Photos</h2>
<p>For a whole library, start with Google Photos. Install the free app on your iPhone, sign in with your Google account, and switch on backup. Pick Original quality, not Storage saver, if you want every pixel kept. The app uploads in the background while the phone charges. When it finishes, open Google Photos on your Android phone with the same account, and the library is there.</p>
<p>The catch is storage. Google gives every account 15 GB free, shared across Gmail, Google Drive and Google Photos. A big photo library will pass that fast. Check how much room your library needs before you start: iPhone Settings shows the size under General, then iPhone Storage. Check how much Google room is left in the Google Photos app, under your profile picture. If you are over the free limit, you can pay for more Google storage, or use the cable method in the next section instead.</p>
<p>One more thing to check: backup takes time. A library of thousands of photos can take a night or two on home Wi-Fi. Plug the phone in, stay on Wi-Fi, and leave the app open now and then so it keeps working. The app shows a count of what is left. Let the upload finish before you wipe the iPhone or hand it on.</p>

<h2>Moving everything while you set up the new phone</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse4.mm.bing.net/th?q=two+phones+joined+by+a+cable&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="two phones joined by a cable" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>If your Android phone is brand new, or just factory reset, use the setup-time transfer. It moves photos, videos, contacts and messages in one pass. Connect the two phones with a cable when the setup asks, or use Wi-Fi if your phone offers it. A cable moves more data types than Wi-Fi. Messages and call history usually need the cable, but exactly what comes across depends on the phone maker. Read the checkboxes the wizard shows before you tap through.</p>
<p>Newer phones add a quicker way. Google&#x27;s upgraded switch method shows a QR code on the Android phone, which you scan with the iPhone. It moves photos, contacts, messages and more over a wireless link, with no cable and no app to install. It is rolling out first on select Pixel and Samsung phones, and it needs a recent Android version. If you do not see a QR code, fall back to the cable or the Android Switch app.</p>
<p>This method only works during setup. Once the Android phone is set up and running, the wizard will not offer it again. That is the mistake people make: they finish setup, then look for the transfer. If you already set the phone up, use Google Photos from the first section, or reset the phone and start again. A reset wipes the new phone, so only do it before you have put anything on it.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Charge both phones and keep them close together.</li><li>Start the Android setup and tap through to the copy step.</li><li>Connect the phones with a USB cable when asked.</li><li>Sign in to your Google account on the Android phone.</li><li>Tick Photos and videos, then let the copy finish.</li></ol>
<h2>If your new phone is a Samsung</h2>
<p>Samsung phones ship with their own mover, called Smart Switch. It is already on the phone, and it moves photos and videos from an iPhone over a cable or Wi-Fi. Open Smart Switch on the Samsung, pick Receive data, then iPhone, and follow the prompts. It will ask for the cable first, then offer Wi-Fi if you would rather not plug in.</p>
<p>Smart Switch has its own limits. It will not move your iMessages or your iTunes music and videos. Photos and videos come across, and so do contacts and calendar events. For the rest, use Google Photos. If you bought a Pixel instead, the built-in Android Switch wizard in the last section is the one to use.</p>
<p>Cable note: new iPhones use USB-C, older ones use Lightning. A USB-C to USB-C cable joins an iPhone 15 or newer straight to most new Android phones. An older iPhone needs a Lightning to USB-C cable instead. Check both ports before you start, so the transfer does not stall at the first step for want of the right cable.</p>
<h2>Your photos live in iCloud, not on the iPhone</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse2.mm.bing.net/th?q=iPhone+settings+screen+showing+iCloud+storage&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="iPhone settings screen showing iCloud storage" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>This is the step where photos get lost. Many iPhones keep only small previews on the device, with the full-size originals in iCloud. The setting is called Optimize iPhone Storage, and it is on by default for a lot of people. A phone-to-phone copy can only move what is actually on the phone. The full files sitting in iCloud do not come across.</p>
<p>Apple offers its own fix. Sign in at privacy.apple.com, choose Request to transfer a copy of your data, then sign in with your Google account and follow the prompts. Apple checks it is really you, then copies your iCloud photo library straight to Google Photos. The copy takes roughly three to seven days, and Apple sends an email when it is done. Your originals stay in iCloud.</p>
<p>Rather than wait, download the originals first. On the iPhone, open Settings, tap Photos, and switch off Optimize iPhone Storage so the phone pulls the full files down. That needs room on the phone and time on Wi-Fi. Once the originals sit on the device, the cable transfer or Google Photos backup will pick them up. Open a few photos after the move to be sure they arrived at full size.</p>
<div class="note warn"><b>Warning:</b> Check Optimize iPhone Storage before you move anything. A copy made from previews leaves the full-size photos behind in iCloud.</div>
<h2>A few photos, or no cloud at all</h2>
<p>For a handful of photos, the simplest tool is the share sheet. Pick the photos, tap Share, and send them to yourself by email, message or a cloud link. It is slow for a library and it can shrink the files, so keep it for a few shots only. A newer option is Quick Share, which on recent Pixel and Galaxy phones can talk to Apple&#x27;s AirDrop. Whether your model is included shows up in the Quick Share settings screen.</p>
<p>A computer works as a go-between if you trust no cloud. On Windows, plug the iPhone in with a cable, open the Photos app, and use Import to copy the originals to a folder. Then plug in the Android phone with a USB-C cable. It shows up on the computer, and you can drag the photos into the DCIM or Pictures folder. The same trick works on a Mac with the Android File Transfer app, or by dragging files in Finder.</p>
<p>Try a flash drive for the same job with no computer. A USB-C flash drive plugs straight into an iPhone 15 or newer, or into the Android phone. Older iPhones need a Lightning adapter for the drive. Copy the photos off the iPhone with the Files app, then plug the same drive into the Android phone and copy them over. It is slow for thousands of photos, but it works anywhere.</p>

<h2>After the move: check nothing got lost</h2>
<p>Do not wipe the iPhone yet. Open Google Photos on the Android phone and scroll through the library. Check the count against the iPhone: the two numbers should match. The Photos app shows the iPhone total at the bottom of the Library tab. Open a few photos and pinch to zoom. A photo that arrived small or blurry probably came across as a preview, not the original. If the count is short, the backup or the transfer is still running, or the iCloud originals never left Apple&#x27;s cloud.</p>
<p>Watch for HEIC. iPhones save photos in HEIC format by default, and older Android phones sometimes show them oddly or not at all. Google Photos handles the conversion for you, so HEIC shots show up fine. If you moved files by cable or drive, open a few HEIC shots on the Android phone to be sure they display. If they do not, convert them on the iPhone first by switching the camera format to Most Compatible.</p>
<p>Albums and edits need a last look. Google Photos keeps your albums, but the cable transfer may flatten them into one long list. Live Photos may arrive as stills, and edits may come across baked in rather than adjustable. None of that loses the picture itself. Once the counts match and the zooms look sharp, back the Android library up, then wipe the iPhone.</p>
<ul><li>Count first: know how many photos the iPhone holds.</li><li>Pick the route: Google Photos for a library, cable for a new phone.</li><li>iCloud check: full originals or previews on the device?</li><li>Last look: albums kept, Live Photos still move.</li></ul>
<div class="note"><b>Check this:</b> Live Photos and albums may not survive the cable route. Check them on the Android phone before you wipe the iPhone.</div>
<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Will my photos lose quality when I move them?</h3>
<p>Not if you pick the right setting. Google Photos at Original quality keeps every pixel. The Apple to Google transfer keeps them too. Quality drops only when you share by message or email, or pick Storage saver, which shrinks large photos to save room.</p>
<h3>Can I transfer photos after the Android phone is already set up?</h3>
<p>Yes, but not with the setup wizard. Once setup is done, that transfer option goes away. Use Google Photos backup instead, or the computer and USB drive methods. Resetting the phone brings the wizard back. Only do that if the new phone holds nothing you would miss.</p>
<h3>Do I need a Google account to move my photos?</h3>
<p>For Google Photos and the setup transfer, yes. Both need a Google account to hold the library. The computer and USB drive methods need none. If you do not have a Google account, make one before you start, or move the files by cable and drive instead.</p>
<h3>What happens to my albums and Live Photos?</h3>
<p>Google Photos keeps albums, and Live Photos usually arrive as a still with a short video attached. A cable transfer may flatten albums into one list, and Live Photos may arrive as stills only. The pictures themselves survive either way. Check the albums after the move and rebuild the ones that matter.</p>
<h3>How long does the move take?</h3>
<p>It depends on the size of the library and the method. Google Photos backup of a few thousand photos can take a night on Wi-Fi. The Apple to Google transfer takes days, because Apple queues it and works through the library in the background.</p>
