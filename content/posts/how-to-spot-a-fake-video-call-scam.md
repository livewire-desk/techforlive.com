<h2>How to spot a fake video call scam</h2>
<p>On 12 November 2026, the FBI published an alert about a scam it calls FaceStealer. North Korean hackers stole more than $400 million in cryptocurrency over 18 months using fake video meetings, the FBI says. BleepingComputer reported the alert the same week. Hackers did not break into video call software. They used trust in a familiar face. This guide shows how to spot a fake video call scam before you join it.</p>
<p>Most targets were people who work with cryptocurrency, plus open source developers whose code many projects rely on. Open source means software anyone can read and reuse. That sounds narrow, but the checks in this guide fit anyone who takes video calls. Those same tricks can reach a job seeker or a small business owner.</p>
<p>Alongside the alert, the FBI says it seized the cloud computers behind the scheme and more than 900 webcam videos of victims. Cloud computers are rented machines that run on the internet. Seizing them slows the scam down, but it does not end it. Those methods are now public, and other criminals can copy them.</p>
<p>The $400 million figure belongs to FaceStealer alone. No victim count has been published, so no victim number appears in this guide.</p>

<h2>How the FaceStealer scam works, step by step</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="/media/woman-waving-at-her-laptop-during-a-video-call-4cd9c3b26a-800.webp" alt="Woman waving at her laptop during a video call" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>Photo: Shixart1985, CC BY 2.0</figcaption></figure>
<p>It starts with a message from an account you already know. According to the FBI, hackers took over accounts on Telegram, LinkedIn, Slack and Discord, then used them to invite victims to a video meeting. A hijacked account is one the attacker has taken over.</p>
<p>That link does not lead to the real Zoom, Teams or Meet. It leads to a copy that looks the same. A copy asks you to wait for the meeting to start. While you wait, it records your webcam without telling you.</p>
<p>When the meeting starts, you see faces you know. They are not live. They are recordings of earlier victims, played back to make you trust the call. AI-made deepfake videos of well-known crypto figures appear in some calls too, the FBI says. A deepfake is a fake video made by software.</p>
<p>Mid-call, the sound cuts out, or an error message appears, and someone on the call offers a fix. It is a file to install, a script to run, or a command to paste into a terminal window. A terminal is a tool for typing text commands to a computer. Running the fix installs malware, which is harmful software. BleepingComputer reports the malware steals crypto data and passwords.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>A hijacked account invites you to a video meeting.</li><li>A link opens a fake copy of Zoom, Teams or Meet.</li><li>Your webcam is recorded while you wait for the start.</li><li>Recordings of people you know play on screen.</li><li>A fake audio glitch appears, and a fix is offered.</li><li>Running the fix installs malware that steals crypto and passwords.</li></ol>
<h2>Five checks before you join any video call</h2>
<p>Check the invite on a second channel first. If it arrived on Telegram, call or text the person on a phone number you already had. If you cannot reach the person, do not join.</p>
<p>Use the installed app instead of the link. Open Zoom, Teams or Meet from your own device and enter the code for the meeting. You can find the code in the invite details, under the link itself. This is the FBI&#x27;s main advice. A meeting that can only be reached through a link in a chat message fails this test.</p>
<p>Read the link address before you click anything. Look at the full address in the message, not just the display text. A real Zoom link starts with zoom.us, and a real Teams link starts with teams.microsoft.com. If the address looks strange, treat the invite as fake.</p>
<p>Leave the camera off when you join. A switched-off camera gives away no video for the scammers to reuse, and it gives you time to judge whether the call is real. Switch it on once a live person has greeted you by name and answered you back.</p>
<ul><li>Confirm the invite on a second channel, with a number you already had.</li><li>Enter the meeting code in the app you installed yourself.</li><li>Read the full link address and look for look-alike spellings.</li><li>Turn the camera on only after the call checks out.</li><li>Ask a question only the real person could answer.</li></ul>
<h2>&#x27;Fix my audio&#x27;: the request that is always the attack</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse1.mm.bing.net/th?q=Error+message+on+a+laptop+screen+during+a+video+call&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="Error message on a laptop screen during a video call" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>No real meeting needs a command from a guest. FBI advice puts it plainly: do not run scripts or copy code into the command line to update the client. If anyone on a call asks you to do that, the call is a scam, whatever face is on the screen.</p>
<p>That request often sounds routine. It may be an update for the meeting app, a driver for your microphone, or a small script that checks your sound. Wording stays calm and helpful on purpose.</p>
<p>Update your apps yourself, on your own time, from the official place. On a computer, that means the app store or the maker&#x27;s own site. On a phone, it means the App Store or Google Play. Never install anything offered during a call, and never paste a command someone sent you in chat.</p>
<p>Watch for pressure too. Several familiar faces may appear at once, all waiting for you to run the fix. That is part of the trick, not proof the call is real. Ask something only the real person would know, then wait for an answer. A recording cannot reply.</p>
<div class="note warn"><b>Warning:</b> Leave the call at once if anyone asks you to run something. Do not click the link first to check it.</div>

<h2>If you already clicked or ran the fix</h2>
<p>Act fast, and do not blame yourself. This scam fooled experienced, security-minded people, the FBI says, and BleepingComputer reports that attackers sometimes waited months before stealing, so balances could grow first. Steps below come from the FBI and from the Federal Trade Commission.</p>
<p>Disconnect the device from the internet but leave it switched on. That is the advice from the FBI&#x27;s crime reporting office, known as IC3. Then stop logging in to accounts from that device. Run a scan with the security software you already have.</p>
<p>Change your passwords from a different, clean device, starting with email and any account that holds money. Turn on two-factor authentication, which asks for a second code when you log in, on every account that offers it. End all active sessions where the account settings allow it.</p>
<p>If you hold cryptocurrency, move it only from a clean device. Set up a new wallet with new recovery phrases, which are the secret words that open a wallet, and transfer your funds there. Do not type the new phrases on the affected device. Report what happened at IC3.gov using the keyword FaceStealer.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Disconnect from the internet, but leave the device switched on.</li><li>Stop logging in to accounts from that device.</li><li>Run a scan with security software you already have.</li><li>Change passwords from a clean device, starting with email.</li><li>Turn on two-factor authentication and end active sessions.</li><li>Warn your contacts, then report at IC3.gov with the keyword FaceStealer.</li></ol>
<h2>Myths you can ignore about fake video calls</h2>
<p>First myth: it is all AI deepfakes. Headlines say deepfakes, but the FBI says most of the faces were real. Hackers recorded real people and played those recordings back. That is why looking closely at the face does not help.</p>
<p>Second myth: only crypto people get targeted. Crypto holders were the first targets, because that is where the money was. According to the FBI, the campaign has spread to open source developers, and the same tricks can reach anyone who takes video calls.</p>
<p>Third myth: you would notice a fake face. You will not notice a recording of your colleague, because it is your colleague. Judge the link, the second channel, and the request to run a fix, not the picture.</p>
<p>Last myth: security software makes you safe. It helps, and you should keep it updated. But this scam does not break in through a hole in your software. It asks you to open the door yourself, by running the fix. Refusing that request is the protection that matters. That habit is free, and it works every time. It costs nothing.</p>
<ul><li>Myth: it is all deepfakes. Fact: most faces were real recordings.</li><li>Myth: only crypto holders are targets. Fact: developers and anyone on video calls can be next.</li><li>Myth: you would spot a fake face. Fact: a recording of a real person looks real.</li><li>Myth: security software stops it. Fact: it stops break-ins, not a fix you run yourself.</li></ul>
<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Can a scammer fake a live video call in real time?</h3>
<p>Mostly they do not need to. FaceStealer plays recordings of real people, which look live because the people are real. AI deepfakes of well-known figures appear in some calls, according to the FBI, but the recordings are the main trick. A live question is the best test, because a recording cannot answer one.</p>
<h3>What if the invite came from my boss&#x27;s real account?</h3>
<p>It may have. Hackers took over real accounts on Telegram, LinkedIn, Slack and Discord, the FBI says. A real account can still send a fake invite. Confirm it on a second channel, such as a phone call to a number you already had, before you join.</p>
<h3>Is Zoom or Teams itself unsafe?</h3>
<p>No. Fake pages only copy the look of Zoom, Teams or Meet. Real apps are not the problem, and joining through the official app is the FBI&#x27;s main advice. Type the meeting code into the app you installed yourself, and do not use the link in the message.</p>
<h3>I ran the fix. Is my money already gone?</h3>
<p>Not always. BleepingComputer reports that attackers sometimes waited months before stealing, so balances could grow first. Act now. Disconnect the device, stop logging in from it, and scan it. Change passwords from a clean device. Move crypto to a new wallet set up on a clean device.</p>
<h3>Should I report a fake invite I did not click?</h3>
<p>Yes. Report it at IC3.gov using the keyword FaceStealer. Reports help the FBI track the campaign, warn others, and find the infrastructure behind it. A report about an invite you ignored is still useful, because it shows where the scam is spreading.</p>
