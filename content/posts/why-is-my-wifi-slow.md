<div class="key"><b>In short:</b> Your Wi-Fi is slow because the signal loses strength on the way to your device, or because too many devices share it. Distance, walls, interference from neighbours and household gadgets, and the wrong band are the usual causes. Test with a cable first: if the wired speed is fine, the fix is in your home, not with your provider.<ul><li>Test wired first: a cable tells you whether the problem is Wi-Fi or your internet plan.</li><li>Near the router, use 5 GHz; far away or behind walls, use 2.4 GHz.</li><li>Put the router high, central and in the open, away from microwaves and metal.</li><li>On 2.4 GHz, use channels 1, 6 or 11, whichever is least busy near you.</li></ul></div>
<h2>Why is my Wi-Fi slow even with a fast plan</h2>
<p>Slow Wi-Fi rarely means a slow internet plan. Your plan speed arrives at the router through a cable. The router then carries it through the air to your phone or laptop. Walls, floors and distance all take a cut on the way. The number on your bill is what reaches the router, not your chair. That gap is where most slow Wi-Fi lives.</p>
<p>Four causes cover most cases. Distance and walls weaken the signal, so a room two floors away gets a fraction of the speed. Interference from neighbours and gadgets crowds the same channels. The wrong band puts a fast device on the slow 2.4 GHz network. Too many devices share one pipe, so a backup or a download can starve a video call. Each cause has a different fix, so find yours first.</p>
<p>One test comes first, before you change anything. The wired test in the next section shows whether the problem is your Wi-Fi or your internet plan. That one answer shapes everything after it. Skip it and you may buy gear for a problem that costs nothing to fix.</p>

<h2>Find out whether the Wi-Fi or the internet is slow</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse1.mm.bing.net/th?q=laptop+connected+to+a+router+with+an+Ethernet+cable&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="laptop connected to a router with an Ethernet cable" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>Plug in a cable and the argument settles in two minutes. Compare the wired number with the wireless one. If the wired number nears your plan speed and the wireless number falls far below it, your Wi-Fi is the problem. If both numbers are low, the problem is the plan, the line or the provider.</p>
<p>Run each test three times and keep the middle number. One test can catch a brief dip or a background update on your own machine. Test at different times of day too: cable and fixed-wireless plans slow in the evening, when the whole street streams. A result low only at 8pm points at the provider or the plan, not your router. Note the upload speed too: video calls need it more than downloads do.</p>
<p>Look at the ping figure the test shows, because it tells you about lag rather than speed. Under 50 ms, a ping feels fine for browsing, streaming and calls. High or jumping ping points at interference or a crowded channel. Close the test, then look at what else is running. Cloud backups, game updates and video calls on other devices can halve the speed you see. Test again with those paused to see the true picture.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Turn off Wi-Fi on the laptop and plug in the Ethernet cable.</li><li>Run a speed test three times and note the middle result.</li><li>Unplug the cable, turn Wi-Fi on, stand next to the router and test again.</li><li>Compare the two numbers: a big gap means the Wi-Fi is the problem.</li></ol>
<h2>Move the router and pick the right band</h2>
<p>Where the router sits matters more than its brand. Wi-Fi signals spread in all directions and weaken with every wall and floor they cross. A router hidden in a cupboard, behind the TV or at the far end of the house starts with a handicap. Move it to a central spot, out in the open, about chest height on a shelf. That one move often lifts speeds across the whole home.</p>
<p>Most routers broadcast two networks, 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz. Some add 6 GHz. The 2.4 GHz band reaches further and passes through walls better, but it is slower and shared with many devices. The 5 GHz band is much faster and less crowded, but its range is shorter. The 6 GHz band is faster still, with the shortest range. Near the router, the higher band almost always feels faster, so connect phones, laptops and TVs to 5 GHz close by.</p>
<p>Keep simple gadgets on 2.4 GHz. Smart plugs, sensors and printers barely use speed, and they work better on the band that reaches further. Save 5 GHz for the devices that need it: streaming, gaming and video calls. If your router merges both bands under one network name, check its settings. Look for a band-steering or smart-connect option, or split the bands into two names so you can choose. The wrong band is one of the most common reasons for slow Wi-Fi.</p>

<h2>Dodge interference and crowded channels</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse3.mm.bing.net/th?q=microwave+oven+next+to+a+Wi-Fi+router+in+a+kitchen&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="microwave oven next to a Wi-Fi router in a kitchen" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>Your Wi-Fi shares the air with everything else that uses radio. Microwaves, baby monitors, cordless phones and Bluetooth gadgets all crowd the 2.4 GHz band. A running microwave can stall a video call in the next room. Neighbouring networks add to the pile, especially in flats, where dozens of routers may sit metres from yours. Each one fights for the same channels, and your speed drops as the noise rises.</p>
<p>Channels are the fix on 2.4 GHz. The band has only three channels that do not overlap: 1, 6 and 11. A Wi-Fi analyser app shows which channels nearby networks use, and your router settings let you pick one. Choose the least busy of the three and test again. On 5 GHz there are many more channels, so congestion is rarer, but the check still helps in a crowded block. Leave the channel on Auto if the choice looks even, because most modern routers pick well on their own.</p>
<p>Move the router away from the worst offenders. Keep it clear of the microwave, the TV and large metal, and never tuck it behind a fish tank: water absorbs Wi-Fi signals. Metal shelves and mirrors bounce the signal in odd directions. A metre of clear air around the router costs nothing and often helps more than a settings change. Test before and after each move so you know what helped.</p>
<h2>Too many devices and too much background traffic</h2>
<p>A home network is a shared pipe, and every device drinks from it. Phones, laptops, TVs, speakers, cameras and smart gadgets all count, and a house can easily pass thirty devices. Most sip, but a few gulp: a cloud backup, a game download or a 4K stream can take the whole pipe. When a call stutters at the same time every evening, look for the backup or update that starts then.</p>
<p>Pause the heavy jobs before you blame the router. Cloud backups and photo syncs can run for hours, and game consoles download updates in the background without asking. Open the backup or console settings and set large uploads to run overnight. On a laptop, check the task manager for the process eating the network. One paused backup has fixed more slow evenings than any new router has.</p>
<p>Many routers let you set priorities, called Quality of Service, or QoS. It tells the router to protect calls and streaming first and let downloads wait. The setting lives in the router admin page, usually at 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.0.1, and the exact address is printed on the router label. Turn QoS on and put video calls at the top. It will not add speed, but it stops one download from wrecking everything else.</p>
<ul><li>Cloud backup: pauses the whole house while it uploads</li><li>Game update: downloads in the background for hours</li><li>4K stream: takes the largest steady share</li><li>Video call: needs little speed but hates delay</li></ul>
<h2>When the router itself is the problem</h2>
<p>Try the oldest fix in the book first: restart the router. Unplug the modem and router, wait 30 seconds, then plug the modem back in first, the router once its lights settle. It clears a memory leak or a stuck process, and it alone fixes many slow evenings. Do this before you spend money. If the router is more than five years old, though, a restart only buys you time.</p>
<p>Check for a firmware update in the router admin page. Makers fix bugs and speed problems in updates; old firmware can leave the router slow for months. Turn on automatic updates if the router offers them. While you are there, look at the Wi-Fi standard on the box. Wi-Fi 4 and Wi-Fi 5 routers are old now, and Wi-Fi 6 handles many devices far better. If wired is fast but wireless is slow on every device, the router is the weak link.</p>
<p>Other faults sit outside your home. Cable plans slow at peak hours, when the whole street streams at once, and fixed-wireless plans share a cell tower the same way. If the wired test is slow only in the evening, ask the provider about congestion. Check for a faster plan or a different connection type. An extender or a mesh set helps only when the Wi-Fi is the weak link, not when the line itself is slow. Buy the fix that matches the cause you found.</p>
<div class="note warn"><b>Common mistake:</b> Do not buy a new router before the wired test. If the cable is slow too, the router was never the problem.</div>
<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Why is my Wi-Fi slow only in one room?</h3>
<p>Distance and walls are the usual reason: the signal weakens with each wall and floor, and 5 GHz fades fastest. Switch that room&#x27;s devices to 2.4 GHz, or add a mesh node halfway between the router and the room. Test the speed in the doorway to find where it drops.</p>
<h3>Does a VPN slow down Wi-Fi?</h3>
<p>Yes, it can. A VPN adds encryption and routes your traffic through another server, which costs speed and adds delay. Turn the VPN off and test again to see how much it costs you. If the drop is large, try a nearer VPN server or keep the VPN off for streaming.</p>
<h3>Will a Wi-Fi extender fix slow Wi-Fi?</h3>
<p>Only when the problem is range. An extender repeats a weak signal, and it halves the speed on that hop. It talks to the router and your device on the same channel. A mesh set does the job better, and moving the router is free. Test the wired speed first: if the line is slow, no extender helps.</p>
<h3>How often should I restart my router?</h3>
<p>Restart it when Wi-Fi turns slow for no clear reason, and once a month as routine. A restart clears stuck processes and frees memory. If you need to restart it every day, the router is failing or its firmware needs an update.</p>
<h3>Is 5 GHz always faster than 2.4 GHz?</h3>
<p>Near the router, yes: 5 GHz is faster and less crowded, so it wins in the same room. Far away or behind thick walls, 2.4 GHz wins because it reaches further. Use 5 GHz close by and 2.4 GHz for distant rooms.</p>
