<h2>What a large language model is, in one idea</h2>
<p>A large language model, or LLM, is a program that writes by guessing. Give it some words, and it predicts the most likely next word. Then it adds that word and guesses again. It repeats that loop until the answer is done. Every chatbot you have used works this way, including ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude.</p>
<p>That sounds too simple to explain a machine that writes essays and code. The trick is the guessing itself. To guess the next word well across billions of sentences, the model has to pick up grammar, facts, style and even rough reasoning. A model that only memorised phrases would fail the first new sentence. A model that learned the patterns can handle sentences nobody has written before.</p>
<p>Hold on to that one idea and the rest of this guide falls into place. The model&#x27;s strengths come from that one trick. So do its failures. When it states a false fact with total confidence, that is the guesser doing its job. It picked the words that sounded most likely, not the words that were true. Knowing that is the key to using one well.</p>

<h2>How a large language model reads your words</h2>
<p>No model sees words the way you do. Before it reads anything, it chops the text into pieces called tokens. Often a token is a whole short word, like &#x27;cat&#x27;, or a chunk of a longer one. The word &#x27;unbelievable&#x27; might arrive as three tokens. Spaces and punctuation get their own tokens too. Everything the model does starts from those pieces.</p>
<p>Tokens matter because they are the unit the model counts. The limit on how much text it can hold at once is counted in tokens, not words. As a rough rule of thumb, 1,000 tokens work out near 750 English words, though it varies with the text. Long words and code eat more tokens than short plain ones. That is why a long chat slowly pushes the earliest messages out of the model&#x27;s view.</p>
<p>You can see tokens at work when a reply goes odd. Ask for a word with a set number of letters and the model may miss, because it never counted letters, only tokens. Ask it to rhyme and it may pick a near miss, because spelling lives inside tokens it cannot split. These are not bugs in one product. They are what you get when a machine reads in chunks instead of letters.</p>
<h2>The transformer: the design that makes it work</h2>
<p>Nearly every chatbot today uses the same basic design, called the transformer. It arrived in a 2017 research paper, &#x27;Attention Is All You Need&#x27;, from a team at Google. Before it, language programs read one word at a time, in order, and forgot the start of a long sentence. The transformer looks at all the words at once. That one change is why today&#x27;s models can hold a whole conversation in view.</p>
<p>Its key move is called attention, or self-attention. For each word, the model asks which other words in the passage matter most for understanding it. In &#x27;the bank by the river&#x27;, the word &#x27;river&#x27; tells the model that &#x27;bank&#x27; means a shore, not a place for money. Each word casts a vote on the words around it. Run that vote across many layers, and the model builds a rich picture of what the sentence means.</p>
<p>Layers stack up. Models run from roughly a dozen layers to nearly a hundred, depending on the model. Each layer refines the picture a little more. The last layer turns that picture into a score for every word the model knows, and the highest scores become the next token. Nothing in here is magic. It is the same loop, run through a design that lets every word weigh in.</p>
<h2>How a large language model learns</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse4.mm.bing.net/th?q=stack+of+books+beside+a+laptop&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="stack of books beside a laptop" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>Every model starts out knowing nothing. Its builders fill it with billions of tiny numbers, called parameters or weights, set at random. Then they feed it huge amounts of text: books, web pages, code, articles. At each step the model guesses the next token, checks the real one, and nudges its numbers a fraction toward the right answer. Repeat that step billions to trillions of times, depending on the model, and the numbers settle into patterns.</p>
<p>Pre-training is the first phase, and it teaches the model language itself. The second phase teaches it manners. Builders show it examples of good answers, and people rank its replies. That teaches it to follow instructions, refuse harmful requests, and say when it does not know. This is why the same base model can feel like a helpful assistant in one product and a raw text machine in another.</p>
<p>Training is slow and dear, while using a trained model is cheap by comparison. That split shapes the whole market. Only a handful of firms train the big models, and thousands of apps build on top of them. Bigger models hold more of those numbers, and that is part of why they cost more to build. It also explains why a model knows nothing after its training ended. Its knowledge stops at the last text it was fed, unless the app around it adds live search or fresh documents.</p>
<h2>What large language models do well, and where they trip</h2>
<p>These models shine at language chores. They draft emails, summarise long documents, explain hard ideas in plain words, and write working code from a plain description. They are good at the first draft, the outline, and the reword. They are also patient tutors: they will re-explain the same point five ways without complaint. For anything that starts as words, they save real time. Some newer models also take in pictures and sound, not just text.</p>
<p>They trip on facts, numbers and fresh events. Quotes, citations and dates can all be invented, then delivered with a straight face. It can also fail simple sums, because it guesses digits the way it guesses words. And it knows nothing about your life, your files or today&#x27;s news unless the app feeds it that context. Treat its output as a starting point, not a source to quote.</p>
<p>The common thread is confidence. A model never says &#x27;I am guessing&#x27; unless it was trained to hedge. It gives the most likely words, and likely words sound sure. That is fine for a poem or a brainstorm. It is a trap for a medical dose, a legal clause or a news fact. Match the job to the tool: use the model to shape words, and use a trusted source to check facts.</p>

<h2>How to use a large language model well</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse1.mm.bing.net/th?q=hands+typing+a+careful+prompt+on+a+laptop&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="hands typing a careful prompt on a laptop" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>Start with a clear ask. Say what you want, who it is for, and what good looks like. &#x27;Explain inflation to a 12-year-old in 100 words&#x27; beats &#x27;tell me about inflation&#x27; every time. Add the facts yourself when they matter: paste the numbers, the dates, the names. The model works best when you give it the raw material and ask it to shape it.</p>
<p>Ask for the shape you need. A list, a table, a short version and a long one: the model will follow the format you name. Then check the result like an editor. Read it once for sense, once for facts, and once for numbers. Fix the parts that are wrong instead of asking for a fresh start each time. Small corrections keep the good parts and teach you what the model does well.</p>
<p>Keep private things out of the prompt. A model may store what you type, and the app&#x27;s rules decide what happens next. Names, addresses, passwords and medical details do not belong in a chat box. If the job needs them, use the app&#x27;s own upload or a local tool instead. The rule is simple: type only what you would not mind a stranger reading.</p>
<ul><li>Clear ask: say what you want, who it is for, and the shape.</li><li>Your facts: paste the numbers and names the answer needs.</li><li>One check: read for sense, facts and numbers before you use it.</li><li>Private data: leave names, passwords and medical details out.</li></ul>
<div class="note"><b>Tip:</b> Ask for the same answer twice when it matters. Two different replies mean the model is guessing, not sure.</div>
<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Is a large language model the same as AI?</h3>
<p>No. AI is the broad field, and a large language model is one kind of AI, built for text. Other kinds of AI recognise faces, drive cars or play chess. When people say &#x27;the AI&#x27; today, they usually mean a chatbot built on a large language model.</p>
<h3>Does a large language model understand what it writes?</h3>
<p>Not the way you do. It tracks patterns in words, not meaning. It can use the word &#x27;sad&#x27; correctly without feeling sad, and it can describe a recipe without ever tasting food. The fluent text looks like understanding, and that is exactly what fools us.</p>
<h3>Why do chatbots make up facts?</h3>
<p>Because they guess the most likely words, not the truest ones. Made-up citations can sound more likely than real ones the model never saw. The model has no fact checker inside it. That is why every factual claim in a reply needs a check against a source you trust.</p>
<h3>Can a large language model learn from my chats?</h3>
<p>It depends on the app, not the model. The model itself does not learn from your messages during a chat. Some apps use chats to improve their products, and most let you switch that off in settings. Check the app&#x27;s privacy settings before you share anything personal.</p>
<h3>What is the difference between an LLM and a search engine?</h3>
<p>Search engines find pages that already exist. A large language model writes new text from patterns it learned. Search gives you sources you can open. Chatbots give you an answer with no sources attached. Use search to check facts and a chatbot to shape words.</p>
