<h2>How to make a website for free in an afternoon</h2>
<p>A free website is a real website. You pick a builder, type your pages, and publish, and the address works for anyone with a browser. The catch is not the site itself. Free plans put the site on the builder&#x27;s subdomain, such as yoursite.wordpress.com, and most of them show the builder&#x27;s ads on your pages. Know that trade before you start, and free is a fair deal.</p>
<p>Four routes cover nearly everyone. Google Sites needs no code at all, and it shows no ads. WordPress.com is the natural home for a blog or a writing site. Wix is the easiest drag-and-drop builder for a visual site. GitHub Pages hosts a site you coded yourself, with a custom domain and free HTTPS. The rest of this guide compares them, then walks through the easiest one.</p>
<p>Start by deciding what the site is for. A one-page site for a club or a CV needs almost nothing. Blogs need new posts every week and a way to organise them. A small shop window needs photos and a contact form. Coded sites need files and a host that serves them. Match the tool to the job, and the free plan will carry you further than you expect.</p>

<h2>What free really means: the subdomain and the ads</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse2.mm.bing.net/th?q=website+address+bar+showing+a+free+subdomain+with+builder+ads&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="website address bar showing a free subdomain with builder ads" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>Your address is the first cost of free. A free site lives on the builder&#x27;s domain, so your address reads yoursite.wordpress.com or yourname.wixsite.com. Visitors can still reach it, and search engines can still list it. But the address tells everyone which builder you used, and it is harder to remember and to print on a card. That is what a custom domain fixes, and it is the first thing worth paying for.</p>
<p>Ads and branding are the second cost. Wix and WordPress.com place their own ads on free sites, and the ads sit where your visitors look. Google Sites shows no ads and no badge, which is rare among free builders. GitHub Pages shows no ads either, because it serves plain files. If the site is for a business or a job search, those ads are the reason to upgrade. If it is for a club or a hobby, they hardly matter.</p>
<p>Limits you meet later are the third cost. Free plans cap storage, so a text site with a few photos fits easily while a big gallery gets tight. WordPress.com free also blocks custom plugins, so you cannot add features the builder did not ship. GitHub Pages suits small sites and is not built for heavy traffic. Read the limits before you build, not after.</p>
<div class="note warn"><b>Warning:</b> A free plan is not a trial. Your site stays up as long as the free tier lasts.</div>
<h2>The four free routes, and who each one fits</h2>
<p>Pick by what you will build, not by which name you have heard. A blog needs posts, categories and a reader flow, and WordPress.com was built for exactly that. One-page sites and club pages need almost nothing, and Google Sites does it in an afternoon with no ads. Visual sites with galleries and sections suit Wix, whose editor is the easiest of the four. Coded sites need a host that serves files, and GitHub Pages does that free with a custom domain.</p>
<p>Here is the honest comparison. Look at the subdomain, the ads, the storage and the custom domain column, and the right row will stand out. Ignore the marketing on each builder&#x27;s site, which hides the limits in small print. Free-tier limits change often, and usually downward, so check the builder&#x27;s pricing page before you commit to one. The route you pick matters less than starting, because moving a small site later is a day&#x27;s work, not a rebuild.</p>
<p>Two other names appear in free lists and deserve a note. Carrd is the best free option for a single page, though custom domains need its paid plan. Cloudflare Pages suits technical sites, with a free tier for static sites, though it needs files rather than a visual editor. Check its published limits before relying on it for a busy site. Some lesser-known builders offer free multi-page hosting with branding on a subdomain. Check the terms before you commit. None of these is wrong. They are simply narrower than the big four.</p>

<h2>The easiest free site: Google Sites, step by step</h2>
<p>Google Sites is the fastest way from nothing to a live page. You need only a Google account. There is no editor to learn beyond dragging sections onto a page, and the result looks clean on a phone without any extra work. Publishing takes one click, and the address works at once. It is the route to pick when the site matters more than the design.</p>
<p>Go in with open eyes: the design limits are real. You cannot reshape a Google Sites page far beyond its templates, and the output looks plain next to a Wix site. There are no plugins and no shop, so a site that needs selling or booking will outgrow it. What you get instead is a site that never breaks, never shows ads, and costs nothing to keep. For a club, a class, a CV or a family page, that trade is a good one.</p>
<p>Connecting a custom domain is the one upgrade that costs nothing to the builder. Google Sites supports custom domain mapping, so follow Google&#x27;s instructions and your registrar&#x27;s steps. The cost is the domain purchase, not the Sites service. Point the domain at Google&#x27;s servers as the instructions say, wait for it to take effect, and the site answers on your own address. It is the cheapest route to a professional-looking address.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Open sites.google.com and sign in with your Google account.</li><li>Click Blank or pick a template, then name the site.</li><li>Add pages from the Pages tab and fill them with text and photos.</li><li>Click Publish, check the address, and share it.</li></ol>

<h2>A free blog: WordPress.com, and what Wix adds</h2>
<figure class="fig"><img src="https://tse4.mm.bing.net/th?q=WordPress.com+dashboard+with+a+new+blog+post+being+written&amp;w=560&amp;h=380&amp;c=7&amp;rs=1&amp;p=0&amp;o=5&amp;pid=1.7" alt="WordPress.com dashboard with a new blog post being written" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>
<p>WordPress.com free is the right home for writing. You get a real WordPress install with modest storage, a choice of free themes, and posts and pages that organise themselves. The address is yoursite.wordpress.com, and WordPress.com ads appear on your pages. You cannot install custom plugins on the free plan, and you cannot use a custom domain until you upgrade. For a blog that publishes every week, those limits rarely bite at first.</p>
<p>Wix free is the pick for a visual site. You choose a template, drag sections into place, and publish without touching code. The free plan gives modest storage and a yourname.wixsite.com address, and Wix ads appear on every page. The editor is the easiest of the free builders, and the result looks polished. The price is the ads and the subdomain, and storage runs out fast once photos pile up.</p>
<p>Both builders want you to upgrade, and both will ask. WordPress.com puts the upgrade in front of you whenever you want a plugin or a domain. Wix asks when you hit the storage cap or want the ads gone. Treat the free plan as a real home, not a countdown: your site stays live and your posts stay yours. Export your posts now and then, so a move later is a copy, not a rewrite. Upgrade when the site earns it, not when a banner tells you to.</p>
<h2>Coded it yourself? GitHub Pages hosts it free</h2>
<p>If you write HTML, CSS and JavaScript, GitHub Pages is the free host that asks the least of you. It serves plain files from a repository, so what you upload is what visitors see. A custom domain and HTTPS cost nothing extra, and the setup takes minutes. GitHub suggests keeping sites reasonably small, and a small site fits easily.</p>
<p>Pages serves static files only, and that is the catch. It runs no server code, so a contact form needs a separate service, and a shop needs a third-party checkout. Your repository must stay public unless you pay for a GitHub plan. Pages is meant for personal, project and documentation sites, not for commercial ventures. For a portfolio or a hand-built site, those limits never show.</p>
<p>When free stops working, pay in this order. First, a custom domain, because it is the one upgrade visitors notice. Second, removing ads, because it makes the site look like yours. Third, more storage, and only when you actually hit the cap. Do not pay for a plan with features you have not missed yet. A free site that works beats a paid site that nobody visits.</p>
<ol class="steps"><li>Create a public repository named username.github.io.</li><li>Add an index.html file with your page.</li><li>Open Settings, then Pages, and turn Pages on.</li><li>Visit username.github.io to see the live site.</li></ol>
<div class="note"><b>Tip:</b> Test the site on your phone before you share it. Most visitors will meet it there first.</div>
<h2>Quick answers</h2>
<h3>Is a free website really free forever?</h3>
<p>Yes, on the plans in this guide. WordPress.com, Google Sites, Wix and GitHub Pages all keep a free tier with no end date. The limits stay, and the builder may tighten them, but the site stays live at no cost. Paying only buys extras.</p>
<h3>Can a free website show up on Google?</h3>
<p>Yes. Search engines list free sites the same way they list paid ones, and a good page can rank well. Custom domains help a little, and clear titles and real content help far more. The subdomain itself is not a penalty.</p>
<h3>Can I sell things on a free website?</h3>
<p>Not easily. Free plans block online shops, and Wix and WordPress.com keep selling behind paid plans. You can still show products and take orders by email or a contact form. For real checkout, budget for a paid plan.</p>
<h3>What happens if I outgrow the free plan?</h3>
<p>You upgrade in place. The site, the posts and the address stay where they are, and the limits lift. On a subdomain, the address stays the same too. Moving to a different builder takes more work, so pick the builder that fits your next year, not just this week.</p>
<h3>Do I need to know code to make a free website?</h3>
<p>No, unless you pick GitHub Pages or Cloudflare Pages. Google Sites, WordPress.com and Wix are all visual editors: you type, drag and publish. Code helps only if you want a design the editor cannot make.</p>
